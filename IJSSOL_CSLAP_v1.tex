\begin{abstract}
In automated pick-and-pass warehouses the conveyor carries each order past fixed stations, so the operational cost driver is the number of stations an order visits rather than picker travel distance. Correlated storage models nonetheless inherit distance-minimisation objectives from manual picking. We reformulate correlated storage assignment to minimise station visits subject to hard per-station slot capacity and workload budgets. The objective is piecewise constant, since an order's visit count changes only when it gains or loses a whole station, and on the instances studied the linear relaxation is too weak to guide branch-and-bound. We build three strategies around that structure: a set-variable formulation, a set-partitioning column generation, and a clustering heuristic. Across 29 benchmark instances under one protocol, all three scale better with catalogue size than a genetic algorithm and simulated annealing taken as literature baselines. Applied to an operating facility with 21,874 products and 26 picking stations, the set-variable formulation removes 13.7\% of station visits in sample and 6.6 to 7.4\% on held-out weeks, every station remaining inside its workload budget. Finally, a bounded reassignment extension quantifies the marginal return of phased industrial adoption.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
Order picking absorbs a large share of warehouse operating cost, so where products sit on the shelves has a direct effect on throughput \citep{xie2024combi}. The correlated storage location assignment problem (CSLAP) chooses that placement by grouping products frequently ordered together, and most CSLAP models pursue that aim by minimising picker travel distance \citep{islam2023}. That objective suits a warehouse where a picker walks the aisles, not one where goods move on a conveyor. In an automated pick-and-pass facility, each customer order is assigned to an initially empty order box that travels along a mechanical conveyor and is diverted to the picking stations required to collect its products. After each station visit, the box merges back onto the main conveyor, and the order is completed once all requested products have been collected. Each diversion adds a fixed mechanical and handling delay and occupies a slot in a finite station buffer, so the cost driver becomes station visits per order together with the balance of workload across zones. We build a CSLAP model on that basis, with total station visits as the objective and no station permitted to exceed its physical capacity or workload budget.

Solving that model at the scale of a real warehouse is difficult for two reasons. The objective counts discrete stops, so its value changes only when a whole order gains or loses a zone and relocating a single product usually leaves it unchanged, so a search steered by the visit count spends most of its moves on a plateau. On the instance family studied here the linear relaxation of the binary programme is meanwhile no stronger than the observation that every order visits at least one station, whatever the correlation structure (Supplementary Section~S5), so branch-and-bound starts from a root bound carrying nothing instance-specific to prune with.

Three solution strategies address that model, each matched to a different operational role.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Strategic re-slotting: a set-variable formulation.} The binary model is recast over one set variable per station, whose partition operators let the search move whole subsets of products without repairing a binary matrix. It is the principal method and the form deployed on the industrial site.
\item \textbf{Fast layouts: a clustering heuristic.} A deterministic graph procedure with scale-relative thresholds groups co-ordered products into communities and places them under both capacity limits in seconds to minutes,with no certificate of quality.
\item \textbf{Bundle-level search: a set-partitioning column generation.} The problem is decomposed into a master problem choosing among whole product bundles, priced over distinct weighted order supports and driven as a price-and-complete matheuristic that returns feasible layouts together with a valid lower bound.
\end{itemize}
The binary encoding of the same model runs alongside them as a representation control, and two metaheuristics established in the CSLAP literature, a genetic algorithm (GA) and simulated annealing with correlated interchange (SA-C), provide the external comparison.

We make three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item We pose CSLAP for pick-and-pass systems with station visits as the objective under hard per-station capacity and workload limits, replacing the travel-distance objective the correlated storage literature inherits from manual picking, and give the model in a binary and a set-variable form (Section~\ref{sec:model}).
\item We build the three strategies above on the structure of that objective and benchmark them on 29 instances under one protocol against GA and SA-C, with the binary encoding kept as a representation control (Sections~\ref{sec:methods} and~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item We validate on an operating warehouse of 21,874 products and 26 stations, where the reduction holds on weeks the layout was not fitted to and every station stays inside its own workload budget, and add a bounded reassignment extension so the gain can be taken in stages (Section~\ref{sec:industrial}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:litreview}
% =====================================================================
In a manual warehouse a picker walks to the shelves, so preparation time follows the distance walked and storage models minimise it \citep{islam2023, wang2020}. \citet{zhang2019cie} model demand correlation explicitly and place co-ordered products near a depot, and \citet{xiao2010correlated} use bill-of-material structure to the same end, again measuring the benefit in distance saved. That objective does not describe an automated pick-and-pass warehouse, where a conveyor carries the order past fixed stations and no picker travel occurs. Where goods move mechanically the limits are instead station visits, order splits, mechanical dwell and the synchronisation of workload across zones \citep{azadeh2019, xie2021}, all originating in the per-diversion setup and in the queueing of finite station buffers, both of which fall when an order visits fewer zones \citep{zhu2021, kim2020}. \citet{dekoster2012zones} set the number of zones in a pick-and-sort system by minimising batch completion time; their decision fixes how many zones there are, ours their content.

Three recent models optimise these discrete interactions directly. \citet{zhu2021} minimise order splits across multiple warehouses by clustering, which maps to grouping products into conveyor zones so an order stays intact. \citet{xie2021} minimise pod-station visits, the delivery of one movable shelf rack to one station and the analogue of our station visit, and prove that fewer visits with dispersed workload raise throughput. \citet{mirzaei2021} propose correlated dispersed storage, spreading correlated items across automated assets to avoid a queue at any single workstation. Reducing visits must therefore be paired with workload balance, modelled explicitly: \citet{tarczynski2023} show balance is a precondition for reducing picking time in pick-and-pass zones, \citet{vanGils2018} show storage assignment and zoning must be optimised together, and \citet{boysen2023} conclude that delivering each tote to a capacity-feasible station without queueing is the main determinant of throughput. Whether balance enters the objective or the constraints is itself a choice: \citet{saylam2023minmax} minimise the maximum completion time across zones, whereas \citet{vanheusden2022workload} find the appropriate measure depends on the managerial reason for balancing rather than the layout. We place the limit in the constraints because each station on an installed conveyor has an engineered daily budget operations treat as hard, so the question is not how evenly work is spread but how few visits are reachable without exceeding it.

Storage assignment under hard synchronisation carries the structure of a constrained partitioning problem for which no polynomial method is known, and decomposition methods have gained ground on it. \citet{muter2022} solve a set-partitioning model of order batching and picker scheduling by column generation with workload pacing inside pricing, and \citet{zhen2025} design column-and-row generation for hard synchronisation in autonomous-robot warehouses; station capacity and workload can therefore be carried as resource limits inside a pricing subproblem, the route we follow. Two known difficulties shape our design: a per-order coverage encoding makes pricing scale with the raw order count, and a station-indexed master over identical stations is invariant under relabelling, a symmetry degrading both the relaxation and the integer search \citep{vanderbeck2000dw}. Our framework prices over distinct weighted order supports and, when stations are interchangeable, aggregates the master so the symmetry never arises. Degeneracy of the covering master is a further difficulty our design does not address: it is usually met with dual stabilisation \citep{dumerle1999stabilized, wentges1997weighted}, and we report where it would help without implementing it.

Adaptive large neighbourhood search \citep{ropke2006alns} and open-source constraint programming over Boolean satisfiability \citep{perron2023cpsat} are closely related paradigms that Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} returns to as the comparisons this study does not make, while learning-based hybrids such as \citet{barnhart2024learnthenoptimize} schedule operations over a short horizon, whereas our decision is the strategic placement those operations inherit. Table~\ref{tab:litreview} positions this paper along the dimensions that matter in the automated setting: several authors already advocate the visit objective, but to our knowledge no study solves it for a real catalogue of tens of thousands of products under hard per-station workload limits, validated on an operating facility.

\section{Problem definition and decision-aid model}\label{sec:model}
% =====================================================================
%\subsection{The pick-and-pass picking section}
Orders travel along a main conveyor and pass a sequence of picking stations, each on a spur off the main line (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}) and each holding a fixed set of products on nearby shelving. When an order needs a product stored at a station its tote (or empty box) diverts onto the spur, an operator picks the required items, and the tote merges back, repeating this at every station holding one of its products before leaving for dispatch. One such diversion is a \emph{station visit}, and an \emph{order split} occurs when an order's products sit across more than one station.

\subsection{Products, orders and station visits}\label{sec:sets}
Let $P$ be the catalogue of products, each one stock-keeping unit (SKU), $O$ the orders observed over the planning horizon, and $S$ the stations. Order $o$ requests the products of its line set $P_o \subseteq P$, and $L_p$ is the number of order lines in which product $p$ occurs. Each station $s$ has $\zeta_s$ storage locations, a processing rate $V_s$ in lines per unit time, and a workload budget $T_s$ in the units of $\sum_{p} L_p/V_s$; the product $C_s := T_s V_s$ is its line capacity, the form the pricing subproblem of Section~\ref{sec:cg} uses. Order $o$ visits station $s$ when at least one product of $P_o$ is stored there, and the number of stations an order visits is the quantity the model counts. Table~\ref{tab:data} lists the problem data; the decision variables are declared with each model below, and the notation of the decomposition method is collected where that method is introduced (Table~\ref{tab:cgnotation}).

\subsection{Objective: minimising station visits}\label{sec:objective}
Two operational facts make the visit count, rather than any distance, the quantity to minimise.

\textbf{Fixed stopping overhead.} A visit carries a fixed cost independent of how many items are picked: the tote must be decelerated, inducted, scanned and merged, and the station display must retrieve the lines to pick before the first item is touched. Grouping co-ordered products at one station collects more items per stop, and one longer stop costs less than several short ones.

\textbf{Buffer decongestion.} Each station holds a finite buffer in which totes queue, and its overflow blocks the main line. Consider a station calibrated for 1,000 lines a day. That workload can reach it in two ways:
\begin{itemize}
\item fragmented picking: 500 orders visit the station for two lines each, so 500 totes pass through its buffer;
\item concentrated picking: 200 orders visit for five lines each, so 200 totes pass through.
\end{itemize}
The picking work is identical and the buffer occupancy 60\% lower. The model does not observe diversion traffic directly, so holding a station's assigned lines within its engineered daily budget serves as the operational proxy.

\subsection{Workload balancing as a hard constraint}\label{sec:workload}
Concentrating picks to cut visits carries a risk of its own. A pick-and-pass line behaves as a network of queues \citep{kim2020, tarczynski2023}: were co-ordered products clustered without limit, the optimisation would route most of the traffic to a few stations, and as the arrival rate of totes at a station approaches its service rate the expected waiting time grows without bound, starving the other stations and blocking the main line. Workload balance therefore enters as a constraint and not as a term of the objective. Each station's assigned load must stay within its budget $T_s$, which operations treat as fixed once the conveyor is installed, and minimising visits under that limit secures the overhead and buffer benefits without creating a local bottleneck. How the budget is measured on the industrial site is stated in Section~\ref{sec:industrial}.

\subsection{Modelling abstraction}\label{sec:abstraction}
We model placement at the station level. A product is assigned to a station without fixing which physical slot it occupies, since shelving within a station can be rearranged without changing $\zeta_s$, and each product occupies one of the station's $\zeta_s$ locations; a per-product footprint would replace that unit count in the capacity constraint without changing the structure of the programme (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}). Capacity is tight throughout the study: the benchmark instances of Section~\ref{sec:experiments} give $\sum_{s} \zeta_s = |P|$ by construction, and on the industrial site of Section~\ref{sec:industrial} each station's capacity is the number of products it already holds, so every location is occupied and a product can move only by exchange. Each product is stored at a single station; duplicating high-demand products across stations is left to Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{Binary mixed-integer linear programme}\label{sec:milp}
We present the model in two equivalent forms. The first uses the standard binary encoding below, whereas the second replaces the binary matrix with set decision variables, an abstraction specific to the Hexaly solver \citep{blaisemodeling} that exposes high-level partition operators to the search (Section~\ref{sec:setvar}). Both encode the same assignment problem.

\noindent\textbf{Decision variables:}
\begin{itemize}
\item $x_{ps}$, binary, equal to 1 when product $p \in P$ is assigned to station $s \in S$;
\item $z_{os}$, continuous in $[0,1]$, recording whether order $o \in O$ visits station $s$; it depends entirely on $x$ and is forced to 1 whenever any product of $o$ sits at $s$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Objective:}
\begin{equation}
\min \quad \sum_{o \in O} \sum_{s \in S} z_{os} \label{eq:obj}
\end{equation}
The objective is the total number of station visits over all orders.

\noindent\textbf{Constraints:}
\begin{align}
& \sum_{s \in S} x_{ps} \ge 1 && \forall p \in P \label{eq:assign}\\
& \sum_{p \in P} x_{ps} \le \zeta_s && \forall s \in S \label{eq:cap}\\
& x_{ps} \le z_{os} && \forall o \in O,\ p \in P_o,\ s \in S \label{eq:link}\\
& \sum_{p \in P} x_{ps}\,\frac{L_p}{V_s} \le T_s && \forall s \in S \label{eq:wl}\\
& x_{ps} \in \{0,1\},\quad z_{os} \in [0,1]. && \label{eq:dom}
\end{align}
Constraint \eqref{eq:assign} requires every product to be stored at some station. Constraint \eqref{eq:cap} caps the number of products a station holds by its slot count. The linking rows \eqref{eq:link} force $z_{os}$ to 1 whenever any product of order $o$ sits at station $s$. The workload cap \eqref{eq:wl} bounds the load a station carries; its sum runs over all products assigned to the station, not over a single order.

Two properties follow from the rows once the tight capacity of Section~\ref{sec:abstraction} is taken into account. First, \eqref{eq:assign} and \eqref{eq:cap} together fix the assignment: summing \eqref{eq:cap} over the stations bounds the total number of placements by $\sum_s \zeta_s = |P|$, while \eqref{eq:assign} demands at least one placement per product, so every product is placed at exactly one station. Second, $z_{os}$ is integral although declared continuous: $x$ being a binary assignment, the linking rows and the minimisation set $z_{os}$ to $\max_{p \in P_o} x_{ps}$ at any optimum, which is 0 or 1. Constraint \eqref{eq:assign} is written as an inequality because it is the form that survives slack capacity, where the same rows admit storing a product at more than one station, the duplication extension of Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

The rows also explain why the relaxation is weak. Summing the linking rows \eqref{eq:link} over the stations and applying \eqref{eq:assign} gives $\sum_{s} z_{os}\ge 1$ for every order, a floor that $x_{ps}=1/|S|$ attains under the operational slack of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, so on this family the linear relaxation is worth exactly $|O|$ whatever the correlation structure (Supplementary Section~S5).

\subsection{Set-variable reformulation}\label{sec:setvar}
The same model admits an alternative representation through set decision variables, a feature of the Hexaly solver that replaces the binary assignment matrix with one set variable per station \citep{blaisemodeling}, whose high-level operators (partition, contains, count) let the solver navigate the flat, step-function landscape without flipping individual binary flags. The set variable $\Phi_s$ holds the subset of products assigned to station $s$, so $\{\Phi_s\}_{s \in S}$ replaces the matrix $x_{ps}$ and the number of declared variables falls from $|P|\times|S|$ to $|S|$. The model reads
\begin{align}
\min \quad & \sum_{o \in O} \bigl|\{\, s \in S \,:\, \Phi_s \cap P_o \neq \emptyset \,\}\bigr| \label{eq:setobj}\\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \{\Phi_s\}_{s \in S}\ \text{is a partition of}\ P, \label{eq:setpart}\\
& |\Phi_s| \le \zeta_s && \forall s \in S, \label{eq:setcap}\\
& \sum_{p \in \Phi_s} L_p / V_s \le T_s && \forall s \in S, \label{eq:setwl}
\end{align}
where \eqref{eq:setobj} counts, for each order, the stations holding at least one of its products, the value $\sum_{s} z_{os}$ takes at optimality in \eqref{eq:obj}, and \eqref{eq:setcap} and \eqref{eq:setwl} restate \eqref{eq:cap} and \eqref{eq:wl} on the partition.

Only the representation changes, and the problem is hard either way: setting the visit objective aside, the slot-capacity rows alone ask for a partition of the catalogue into $|S|$ blocks of bounded size under a bounded per-block load, the structure of multiway number partitioning \citep{garey1979computers}, so we expect no polynomial method and claim no reduction here. What changes is the burden on the search: \eqref{eq:assign} is not written at all, since a partition satisfies it by construction, so single-station assignment becomes a property of the representation rather than something the search must repair. We solve both forms with Hexaly and call them the binary MILP and the set-variable formulation; on a few thousand products the two perform alike, and the reformulation separates only at industrial scale (Section~\ref{sec:industrial}).

\section{Solution methods}\label{sec:methods}
% =====================================================================
Three solution strategies address the model, and all three confront the same obstacle: a single-product move seldom changes the visit count, so each strategy is defined by the unit of change it searches with, a whole partition, a community of co-ordered products, or a station-sized bundle. The set-variable formulation is stated in Section~\ref{sec:setvar} and solved as written, so this section develops the two other strategies, the common initialisation and the baselines; the binary encoding of Section~\ref{sec:milp} runs alongside as a representation control, not a fourth strategy. The binary and set-variable forms consume the largest budget and are compared against each other on the same engine under the same cap, which isolates the effect of the decision representation while holding the search technology fixed. The clustering heuristic returns a feasible layout in minutes with no certificate of quality, whereas the set-partitioning column generation searches at the level of whole product bundles and returns a stream of feasible layouts with a lower bound it reports as a diagnostic. The GA and SA-C baselines of Section~\ref{sec:baselines} provide the external comparison.

\subsection{Fast feasible layouts: greedy clustering heuristic}\label{sec:heuristic}
For catalogues of this size we use a fast heuristic that groups strongly co-ordered products into communities and places them on stations under both capacity constraints, extending the agglomerative idea of \citet{chuang2016} but building disjoint communities by greedy graph connectivity. Three thresholds filter the correlation graph and a fourth bounds the communities built on it. The filters are indexed on catalogue volume $N=|P|$, so a pair count that signals affinity at 50 products is noise at 20,000, whereas the bound is indexed on the slot capacity of a station, because a community must fit inside one. That fourth rule therefore presumes the stations are alike; where capacities differ widely no single number describes the room any of them has, and Section~\ref{sec:industrial} reports what it costs on such a site.
\begin{itemize}
\item $\phi=\max(2,\lfloor N/100\rfloor)$, the frequency floor: a product enters the graph only if it appears in at least $\phi$ orders, anchoring the noise floor at 1\% of catalogue volume.
\item $\gamma=\max(2,\lfloor N/50\rfloor)$, the co-occurrence floor: a pair enters only if its products are ordered together at least $\gamma$ times.
\item $r=0.1$, the kept-edge ratio: a pair survives only if its co-occurrence reaches a tenth of the more frequent endpoint's own demand, removing edges inflated by one ubiquitous endpoint.
\item $\beta=\max\!\left(5,\big\lfloor 0.4\,\zeta+\tfrac12 \big\rfloor\right)$, the community size bound, where $\zeta=|P|/|S|$ is the slot capacity of a station: it prevents clusters no station can hold.
\end{itemize}
The coefficient $0.4$ is calibrated on a separate family of 80 instances in which slot capacity and catalogue size vary independently, which the benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:experiments} cannot do because its generator ties the station count to the catalogue. There the visit-minimising bound tracks the slot capacity and is insensitive to the catalogue (Supplementary Section~S1), and rescaling any of the four thresholds moves the result little (Supplementary Section~S2), so we keep the defaults and tune none per instance.

Five stages take the orders to a layout (Figure~\ref{fig:heuristic_flow}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Preprocessing and pair generation.} Products appearing in fewer than $\phi$ orders are set aside. Every unordered pair of retained products sharing a multi-item order is counted, $\mathrm{cnt}_{ij}$ being the number of orders containing both. Pairs with $\mathrm{cnt}_{ij}<\gamma$ are dropped, as are pairs whose count falls below the fraction $r$ of the more frequent endpoint's own order count, a ratio symmetric in the two products that reads as the confidence of the weaker association direction. The surviving pairs are sorted by decreasing count.
\item \textbf{Community formation.} While candidate pairs remain, the top pair opens a community, which absorbs the unassigned product of highest aggregate co-occurrence with its current members until it holds $\beta$ products or no adjacent unassigned product is left. Every remaining pair containing an absorbed product is deleted, so no product joins two communities.
\item \textbf{Post-processing.} Each product left unassigned joins, among the communities still below $\beta$, the one with which its co-occurrence is largest, counted on every pair and not only on the filtered graph, ties going to the community opened earliest. Products with no co-occurrence to any open community are grouped into new communities of at most $\beta$ products.
\item \textbf{Station assignment.} A station is admissible for a community when it has slot room for the whole community and its workload $\mathrm{load}(s)$ plus the community's stays within $T_s$. Communities are placed in decreasing order of cumulative demand, each at the admissible station holding the largest share of its products in the starting layout, ties settled towards the lower load ratio $\mathrm{load}(s)/T_s$; a community no station admits whole is split, heaviest product first, each product placed by the same rule. The starting layout is the live layout on the industrial case and the longest-processing-time (LPT) start of Section~\ref{sec:init} on the synthetic instances.
\item \textbf{Workload repair.} Because capacity is tight (Section~\ref{sec:abstraction}), workload is repaired by exchanging products rather than relocating them. The stage repeatedly takes the station of greatest relative overload $\mathrm{load}(s)/T_s$, offers one of its heaviest products in exchange for a lighter one at a station that stays inside its own cap, and among the admissible exchanges applies the one that separates the least co-occurrence weight, so feasibility is recovered at the least cost to the clustering. It stops when every station is inside its cap, when no admissible exchange remains, or when an exchange budget proportional to the catalogue is spent. That is a stopping rule and not a feasibility guarantee, though every run reported here ended with no station over its cap.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Bundle-level search: set-partitioning column generation with a price-and-complete drive}\label{sec:cg}
The obstacle of Section~\ref{sec:methods} is the unit of change. Station visits move only when a whole order gains or loses a station, so a search that relocates one product at a time usually reads no change, whereas a method whose smallest move is a whole group registers where a single-product move does not. A set-partitioning decomposition supplies that unit: its columns are complete product bundles, each a set a station could hold, and choosing one installs the group in a single step. We build it following large-scale pricing methods for logistics and scheduling \citep{lubbecke2005selected, muter2022}, with a \emph{master problem} choosing among the bundles generated so far and \emph{pricing} searching for a new bundle worth adding. Table~\ref{tab:cgnotation} collects the notation of this section.

\subsubsection{Station-indexed and aggregated masters}\label{sec:cgmaster}
The problem decomposes by station. A \emph{pattern} is a set of products a single station could hold, and $K_s$ is the family feasible for station $s$: subsets $q \subseteq P$ with $|q| \le \zeta_s$ and $\sum_{p \in q} L_p / V_s \le T_s$. An order enters the model only through the products it requests, since those decide which stations it visits, so two orders requesting the same products are interchangeable here and we keep one record per distinct product set rather than one per order. Such a set is a \emph{support}: $U$ collects the distinct product sets observed over the horizon, and $w_u$ is the number of orders that requested exactly the set $u$, so a set requested by one order carries $w_u = 1$ and a set requested by a thousand carries $w_u = 1{,}000$. Single-product orders stay out of $U$, since they visit one station under every layout and add a constant no layout can change. A pattern \emph{touches} a support when it holds at least one of its products, so the pattern cost $c_q = \sum_{u \in U} w_u\,\mathbf{1}[q \cap u \neq \emptyset]$ is the number of multi-item orders that would visit a station holding $q$. With one column per station-pattern pair, the linear master reads
\begin{align}
\min \quad & \sum_{s \in S} \sum_{q \in K_s} c_q\, y_{sq} \notag\\
\text{s.t.} \quad
& \sum_{s \in S} \sum_{q \in K_s:\, p \in q} y_{sq} \ge 1 && \forall p \in P \quad (\sigma_p \ge 0) \label{eq:covergen}\\
& \sum_{q \in K_s} y_{sq} \le 1 && \forall s \in S \quad (\mu_s \le 0) \label{eq:convgen}\\
& 0 \le y_{sq} \le 1 && \forall s \in S,\ q \in K_s. \notag
\end{align}
Constraint \eqref{eq:covergen} covers every product and the convexity rows \eqref{eq:convgen} select at most one pattern per station, with the dual of each family in parentheses. For any integer solution whose patterns partition the catalogue the objective equals the station visits of the multi-item orders exactly, and adding the singleton constant gives the total. Pattern feasibility is enforced inside the pricing subproblem of Section~\ref{sec:cgpricing}, one model serving every station.

When every station shares the same capacity $\zeta$, speed $V$ and workload cap $T$, the families $K_s$ coincide, so two solutions differing only in station labelling describe the same physical layout and the master cannot separate them \citep{vanderbeck2000dw}, the relaxation degenerating while the integer search revisits relabelled partitions. An aggregated master removes that symmetry by construction. With identical stations and capacity tight, $|P| = |S|\times\zeta$, a column needs no station index: over a pool $Q'$ of \emph{bundles}, which are patterns carrying no station label, the covering rows \eqref{eq:covergen} are unchanged and the $|S|$ convexity rows \eqref{eq:convgen} collapse to
\begin{equation}
\sum_{q \in Q'} y_q \le |S| \qquad (\mu \le 0),
\label{eq:card}
\end{equation}
taking the master from $|P|+|S|$ rows to $|P|+1$ and removing the orbital symmetry and the per-station multiplicity of pricing at source. Under tight capacity the covering rows act at integrality as an exact partition into $|S|$ full bundles; where capacity is slack they still admit an exactly-once optimal solution, since the objective never gains from placing a product in two bundles, and any surviving double selection is repaired at no cost by Stage~3 of the drive (Section~\ref{sec:cgdrive}).

\subsubsection{Pricing subproblem}\label{sec:cgpricing}
One subproblem generates improving bundles. With $a_p \in \{0,1\}$ marking the products of a candidate bundle and $z_u \in [0,1]$ the supports it touches (the pattern-level analogue of $z_{os}$), the most negative reduced cost solves
\[
\begin{aligned}
\min\ \sum_{u \in U} w_u\, z_u - \sum_{p \in P} \sigma_p\, a_p - \mu
\qquad \text{s.t.} \quad & z_u \ge a_p\ \ \forall u \in U,\ p \in u, \qquad \sum_{p \in P} a_p \le \zeta_s, \\
& \sum_{p \in P} L_p\, a_p \le C_s, \qquad a_p \in \{0,1\},\ z_u \in [0,1].
\end{aligned}
\]
Encoding coverage per distinct support rather than per order keeps the subproblem's size tied to the number of distinct supports, not the raw order count, which grows without bound as the horizon lengthens. What limits the method is the number of pricing iterations reached inside the cap, not the cost of building the model (Section~\ref{sec:cgbound}); Supplementary Section~S4 gives the build and solve costs.

\subsubsection{Price-and-complete drive}\label{sec:cgdrive}
Standard column generation adds one bundle per iteration, the one with the most negative reduced cost. In preliminary runs that loop stalled: because the master selects at most $|S|$ bundles that must together cover every product, a newly priced bundle cannot enter an integer solution until complementary bundles covering the rest of the catalogue are present, so the restricted master ceased to improve even though bundles of negative reduced cost kept arriving. We therefore drive it as a matheuristic generating coordinated bundle sets that form full feasible partitions, the exact pricing subproblem serving to discover mathematically optimal bundles that break the plateau, not to certify optimality. It maintains a pool $Q'$ of every bundle generated, never pruned, and the best complete feasible layout found so far, $\Pi^{\ast}$, writing $\Pi(s)$ for the products a layout $\Pi$ places at station $s$ and $B$ for the time cap. Algorithm~\ref{alg:cgdrive} states the four stages: a warm start from the LPT partition of Section~\ref{sec:init}, a generation loop alternating master solves, pricing passes and completions that enter the pool only if they hold every $\zeta_s$ and $T_s$, an integer master over the accumulated pool, and a closing descent. Four operators do the work.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{\textsc{GreedyPrice}.} Before the exact pricer is called, an accelerator builds one bundle from the products of positive dual price, ordered by price and by price per unit of workload, extending it under the slot and line limits and keeping the prefix of least reduced cost rather than the filled bundle. It returns nothing when no prefix prices out negatively, and its purpose is to supply an improving column at a fraction of the cost of an exact solve.
\item \textbf{\textsc{ExactPrice}.} It solves the pricing subproblem of Section~\ref{sec:cgpricing} as an integer programme under a per-call time limit and returns the bundles found, the solver's dual bound, which remains valid when the call is truncated, and a flag recording whether it proved optimality. Only a proven-empty call may stop the loop, and the dual bound feeds the lower bound of Section~\ref{sec:cgbound}.
\item \textbf{\textsc{Complete}.} It extends a priced bundle to a full layout by placing the remaining products, heaviest first, at the station that admits each under both caps and whose products already touch the greatest weight of supports containing it. A product no station admits goes to the least loaded station, which can leave the completion infeasible for the gate of Stage~2 to discard; the point of completing is that a coordinated set of bundles, and not an isolated column, enters the pool.
\item \textbf{\textsc{Descend}.} It exchanges two products between stations, the same move the workload repair of Section~\ref{sec:heuristic} uses and the only one tight capacity leaves, but here the aim is fewer visits rather than a feasible workload. It takes the first exchange that lowers the visit count plus $\rho$ times the workload carried above the caps, $\rho$ being large enough that no exchange is made for the sake of fewer visits at the cost of feasibility. An exchange is scored from counts kept per station rather than by going through the orders again, and the descent runs on the warm start, on each completed layout and on the best layout at the end.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Price-and-complete drive for the set-partitioning master}
\label{alg:cgdrive}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} products $P$, weighted supports $U$, stations $S$, time cap $B$
\State $\rho\gets\sum_{u\in U}w_u$ \Comment{penalty rate used by \textsc{Descend}}
\Statex
\State \textbf{Stage 1: warm start}
\State $\Pi\gets$ LPT partition of $P$ over $S$ (Section~\ref{sec:init}); $\Pi\gets\Call{Descend}{\Pi}$
\State $Q'\gets$ patterns of $\Pi$ and of 20 mutated variants, each displacing a random 5 to 10\% of the
\State \hskip 1.2em assignments and replacing them greedily, dropping any product left unplaceable
\State $\Pi^{\ast}\gets\Pi$
\Statex
\State \textbf{Stage 2: generation loop}
\While{budget remains}
  \State solve the linear master over $Q'$; read the duals $\sigma_p$ and $\mu$ (or $\mu_s$) \Comment{$\mu_s$: station-indexed}
  \State $Q^{g}\gets\Call{GreedyPrice}{\sigma}$; $(Q^{e},\ \mathrm{rc}_{\mathrm{lb}},\ \textit{opt})\gets\Call{ExactPrice}{\sigma,\mu}$ \Comment{greedy bundle; exact bundles, dual bound, optimality flag}
  \If{\textit{opt} \textbf{and} $Q^{e}=\varnothing$}
    \State \textbf{break} \Comment{proven: no bundle of negative reduced cost exists}
  \EndIf
  \ForAll{bundles $q\in Q^{g}\cup Q^{e}$}
    \State $\Pi_q \gets \mathrm{Descend}(\mathrm{Complete}(q))$
    \If{$|\Pi_q(s)|\le\zeta_s$ and $\sum_{p\in\Pi_q(s)}L_p/V_s\le T_s$ for every $s\in S$}
      \State $Q'\gets Q'\cup$ patterns of $\Pi_q$
      \State \textbf{if} $\Pi_q$ has fewer visits than $\Pi^{\ast}$ \textbf{then} $\Pi^{\ast}\gets\Pi_q$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State update the lower bound (Section~\ref{sec:cgbound})
\EndWhile
\Statex
\State \textbf{Stage 3: integer master}
\State solve the master over $Q'$ with binary variables
\State keep a product selected in two bundles at the station whose other products share the most order weight with it \Comment{see Section~\ref{sec:cgmaster}}
\State $\Pi^{\ast}\gets$ the better of that layout and $\Pi^{\ast}$
\Statex
\State \textbf{Stage 4: final polish}
\State $\Pi^{\ast}\gets\Call{Descend}{\Pi^{\ast}}$
\State \textbf{Output:} layout $\Pi^{\ast}$, its visits recounted on the raw orders, and the lower bound
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Supplementary Section~S4 gives the exact rule of each operator, the incremental swap evaluation and the division of the cap $B$ across the stages.

Three design points are not visible in the stage list. First, Stage 2 resolves the stall because the patterns of the \emph{whole completed layout} enter the pool, so the complementary columns the integer master needs arrive together rather than one at a time. Second, the layout kept is feasible by construction: the gate of Stage~2 admits a completed layout only when it holds every $\zeta_s$ and $T_s$, so $\Pi^{\ast}$ can only improve. Third, Stage 3 lets the integer master reassemble a layout from bundles drawn from different completions, which no single completion could produce.

The master objective counts orders through their supports and leaves out the single-product orders, so the visits reported for a returned layout are recounted on the raw orders under the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\subsubsection{Lower bound and scope}\label{sec:cgbound}
The method also reports a lower bound, a visit count no layout can beat, which shows how much room a returned layout might still leave. It comes from the linear master and the pricing solve rather than from the search, so it holds whatever produced the layouts. The usual bound of a column generation is the value of the linear master, but that is available only once pricing has proved no improving bundle remains, which no run here achieves inside its time cap. A Farley bound is what stands in for it \citep{farley1990note}: it takes the master value as it stands and subtracts the most that the bundles not yet generated could still be worth. Supplementary Section~S5 derives it for both forms of the master.

That subtraction decides whether the bound says anything, and it grows with the instance, since the further pricing stopped from exhausting the improving bundles, the more it removes. At 50 SKUs enough survives it to be informative. At 500 and above it swamps the master value, so the bound is still computed and still valid but sits far too low to say anything about the layouts obtained (Supplementary Section~S8). What that calls for is dual stabilisation rather than a stronger formulation (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}). We therefore report the bound as a diagnostic and not a certificate, and Section~\ref{sec:experiments} measures quality against a per-instance reference instead of a certified optimum.

\subsection{Initialisation and warm start}\label{sec:init}
A warm start must be feasible before it can be good, which rules out the obvious candidate: the clustering heuristic groups products by affinity first, so a layout it returns can leave a station over its workload budget, and a solver handed an infeasible initial assignment discards it. We therefore build the start by a rule that disregards correlation, sorting products by descending order-line frequency $L_p$ and assigning them greedily to the station with the lowest accumulated workload, the LPT sweep of Algorithm~\ref{alg:cgdrive}, which tests both $\zeta_s$ and $T_s$ before each placement. Ignoring correlation is also what makes the layout usable as the correlation-blind anchor of Table~\ref{tab:reliability}, which measures what the catalogue costs when nothing is grouped.

All three strategies and the binary control start from that layout on the synthetic benchmark, so the quality difference is isolated to the search procedure. The two literature baselines keep their own prescribed initialisation, since reproducing them faithfully is the reason for including them: SA-C from a cube-per-order-index (COI) layout \citep{kim2012} and the genetic algorithm from a random capacity-feasible population \citep{pan2015}. On the industrial site every method starts from the layout the site operates today, so a reported reduction is measured against the configuration in use.

\subsection{Baselines}\label{sec:baselines}
We benchmark against two metaheuristics established in the CSLAP literature: simulated annealing with correlated interchange \citep{kim2012, zhang2019cie} and a genetic algorithm \citep{pan2015}. SA-C draws a pair of frequently co-ordered products, exchanges the stations they sit at, and accepts a worsening move with a probability that falls as the temperature cools. The genetic algorithm carries a population of complete assignments, combines two parents by copying a block of the catalogue from one into the other, mutates by exchanging two products' stations, and repairs any station left over capacity.

Both are adapted in one respect. The visit objective is piecewise constant, so most local swaps leave it unchanged and a search guided by it alone stalls on a plateau. Both baselines therefore evaluate a surrogate adding capacity and workload penalties to the visit count together with a dispersion term penalising the separation of correlated items; that third term breaks the plateau, keeping the search moving while the visit count stands still. The penalty and dispersion weights were fixed in advance, not tuned, and we did not search over them, so what the baselines report is the published operators under one untuned constraint-handling scheme. Supplementary Section~S4 gives the operators, weights and parameters of both.

\subsection{Protocol and metrics}\label{sec:protocol}
Every method is run on all 29 instances of Table~\ref{tab:families}, each generated on its own seed, under that table's per-size time cap, so every ranking below is conditional on that cap. One run is reported per instance. The genetic algorithm and the column-generation drive are both stochastic, so their run-to-run variance is not measured here, and a difference at the two larger sizes, where the instances are few, rests on single draws. All runs were produced on one 16-core host under one Python 3.10 environment, each method receiving four threads: the column generation on CPLEX 22.1.1, the two MILP representations on Hexaly 13.0, and the two baselines as our own Python implementations of the published operators. Supplementary Section~S4 states the setup, stage budgets, baseline parameters and seeds in full.

The set-variable formulation is the per-instance reference because it is the form deployed on the industrial site (Section~\ref{sec:industrial}). Every method returned a feasible layout on all 29 instances, holding both the slot capacity and the workload cap, so the comparison below is one of quality and time alone.

Instances differ by two orders of magnitude in difficulty, so a comparison of averages would confound the difference between methods with the difference between instances. We therefore compare them instance by instance under the two-sided exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test \citep{wilcoxon1945}, the standard choice across benchmark instances \citep{demsar2006}; the Gap column of Table~\ref{tab:reliability} carries the size of a difference and the test how reliably it recurs, with the full test table in Supplementary Section~S3. Reporting follows the conventions for computational experiments with heuristics \citep{barr1995designing, hooker1995testing}.

\textbf{The paired tests separate the methods at the two smaller sizes only.} The intervals in Table~\ref{tab:reliability} span 10 to 20\% because the instances differ that much in difficulty, whereas the differences between methods are 0.5 to 2\%, so the paired tests carry the comparison (Supplementary Section~S3). Both literature baselines and the clustering heuristic separate from the reference at 50 and 500 SKUs, the column generation at 50 SKUs only, and the two MILP representations nowhere once the tests are corrected for multiplicity. At 1{,}000 and 2{,}000 SKUs four and three paired instances cannot reach $p<0.05$ whatever the outcome, so the win counts carry the evidence there.

\textbf{The binary and set-variable forms are not separable on this family.} Given the same solver and cap, the two representations land within 0.6\% of each other at every scale, the binary form returning fewer visits on 13 of the 29 instances. The largest synthetic instance declares a $2{,}000 \times 20$ assignment matrix, small enough that whatever the set encoding saves by never repairing constraint~\eqref{eq:assign} stays inside run-to-run noise; the industrial instance, an order of magnitude larger, is where the two forms separate (Section~\ref{sec:industrial}).

\textbf{The column generation overtakes the reference as the catalogue grows.} It trails at the two smaller sizes, matches the reference at 1,000 and leads at 2,000, where it returns fewer visits on every instance (Figure~\ref{fig:crossover}). The mechanism is the bundle-sized unit of change of Section~\ref{sec:cg}, worth little on a small catalogue and decisive on a large one. The 2,000-SKU block holds three instances, so the crossing is an observation on this benchmark and not a threshold we can locate; pooling across sizes does not settle it either, since the mean per-instance gap and the summed visits rank the two methods in opposite orders.

\textbf{Both literature baselines trail, and the objective is one plausible explanation.} Their gap to the reference widens with size (Table~\ref{tab:reliability}), and neither returns fewer visits than the reference on a single instance. Objective, initialisation, constraint handling and implementation are not isolated here: the baselines keep their own prescribed starts, reach feasibility through a penalty and not structurally, and are our own Python implementations against two commercial engines, so part of the deficit may be ours rather than theirs. What weakens with scale is the conversion between the surrogate and the visit count, since a swap changes that count only when it removes an order's last product from a station or carries its first to a new one, and most swaps do neither.

\textbf{The heuristic's trade of quality for time reverses at 2,000 SKUs.} It trails at 50 SKUs and at 2,000 returns fewer visits than the reference on all three instances, in under a minute against twenty. The composition of the move explains it: the heuristic commits a whole community of co-ordered products to a station in one step, so a single step can remove an order's last product from a station, whereas the genetic algorithm's crossover copies a block drawn without reference to co-occurrence and repaired for capacity, which buys no more of this objective than a single-product swap. The same three-instance caveat as above applies.

\section{Industrial application}\label{sec:industrial}
% =====================================================================
We validate the framework on a real warehouse, Company~A. Its decision pool is as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item 26 stations, of which 24 are evaluated. Stations S25 and S26 hold 1 and 2 locations, too few for a reassignment to change the visit count and too few to leave the workload anywhere to move, so they are left as they stand.
\item $|P| = 21{,}874$ products on the 24 evaluated stations, all of which enter the evaluation.
\item $5{,}899$ products frozen in place. A product keeps the location the site has already given it when it appears in at most five orders \emph{and} sits at one of the three static-shelving stations, whose slotting is not re-planned; a further 171 products, which survive only in orders the extract does not retain, are pinned the same way.
\item $15{,}975$ products free to relocate, the pool the solvers receive.
\end{itemize}
Frozen products keep their slots and their workload share, so the solver receives only the remaining tolerance while the evaluation charges every station with its complete load on the complete order stream; the reductions below are therefore not inflated by the reduced decision pool. Supplementary Section~S6 records the dataset, the residual resources and the site rules that fix them.

The Company~A extract is a non-public dataset. It consists of anonymised commercial operational records, order lines and station assignments, supplied by the partner company and used with its permission. It contains no personal data, and the study is non-interventional and involves no human or animal subjects, so no ethics approval was required.

Workload is measured as load, the lines a station handles divided by its own calibrated speed $V_s$ as in \eqref{eq:wl}, which makes the quantity comparable across stations whose processing rates differ by three orders of magnitude, and each station's budget $T_s$ is $110\%$ of the complete legacy load it carried, frozen and movable products together. Because those budgets differ from station to station, Table~\ref{tab:industrial} reports, alongside the busiest-station load, each station's utilisation, its realised load as a percentage of its own legacy load, and the standard deviation of those percentages over the 24 evaluated stations. That denominator gives the legacy layout zero dispersion by construction, so the metric reads as departure from the balance the site operates.

\textbf{The methods separate on workload before they separate on visits.} The heuristic removes 6.2\% in thirteen minutes, but only by spending the tolerance in full: most stations end at or near the $110\%$ cap and several are stripped well below their legacy load (Figure~\ref{fig:workload}a), and its utilisation dispersion of $27.4$ is an order of magnitude above every optimising method. The column generation removes $5.8\%$ while holding dispersion at $2.4$ and leaving headroom everywhere, and its visit count falls inside the spread the heuristic covers over feasible bounds (Supplementary Section~S7), so visits do not separate the two and workload does. A layout pinned against the cap is feasible on the weeks it was fitted to and infeasible on the first week demand grows.

\textbf{On heterogeneous stations the community bound is selected on a grid.} The rule of Section~\ref{sec:heuristic} presumes interchangeable stations; here they hold between 8 and 2{,}575 movable products, and the rule's value on the mean capacity, $\beta=266$, is feasible but not the best reachable. Feasibility is not an interval in $\beta$ (Supplementary Section~S7), so we evaluate a seven-point grid anchored on the mean capacity and keep the feasible layout with fewest visits, $\beta=333$, which gives the heuristic row of Table~\ref{tab:industrial} with the whole grid charged to its time. Every setting from 200 upward is feasible and their reductions span barely a percentage point, so the grid buys avoidance of the infeasible band rather than a tuned bound. On interchangeable stations none of this arises, the rule's $\beta$ being feasible on all 29 benchmark instances.

\textbf{The set-variable formulation removes 13.7\% within the tolerance.} Every station's utilisation shift stays within $[-5.9\%, +9.6\%]$, inside the site's 10\% slack (Figure~\ref{fig:workload}b), so the deepest reduction we obtain is also deployable, although no method here returns a certified optimum. The binary MILP, on the same engine and budget, is feasible everywhere yet carries 7.8\% more visits. Engine and budget being held fixed, the representation is the treatment; the search was not instrumented, so attributing the gap to the $|P| \times |S|$ repair burden the set encoding removes remains an interpretation, and one the synthetic family, where the two forms never differ by more than 0.6\%, gave no warning of.

\textbf{The column generation disturbs the balance least.} It ran on Company~A in its station-indexed form with the set-variable formulation's inputs, so the two solve the same constrained problem, and it removes 5.8\% of the visits while tracking the legacy balance most closely (Figure~\ref{fig:workload}c). Its drive differs from the homogeneous one in three respects, warm-starting from the live layout, installing each priced bundle by ejection and recompletion of the target station, and adding a bound pass before the integer master (Supplementary Section~S4). The profile follows from the drive: starting from the live layout keeps the search near that balance, and enforcing the workload cap inside pricing means no violating bundle is ever generated.

\textbf{Each eliminated visit frees dwell time in proportion to the site's cycle time.} The site runs about 81,700 station stops a week across the extract, so at the 6.6 to 7.4\% the layout holds on unseen weeks (Section~\ref{sec:temporal}) the expected saving is roughly 5,400 to 6,050 stops per week. The duration of a stop cycle is site-specific and unmeasured here, so we state the effect per unit: for every second of per-visit cycle time the out-of-sample reduction frees 1.5 to 1.7 hours of station dwell per week. The projection is linear and ignores queueing, so a discrete-event study is needed to confirm it (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsection{Robustness on unseen weeks}\label{sec:temporal}
A layout is optimised once and then operated as demand evolves, so we test out of sample. The dataset spans 13 weeks, from 1 September to 30 November 2021, split on the recorded delivery date of each order. Two splits are run, each layout compared with the original placement and with an oracle optimised on the test weeks themselves \citep{lee2020cie, wang2020}:
\begin{itemize}
\item the model sees the first 10 weeks and the layout is evaluated on the last 3;
\item the model sees the first 8 weeks and the layout is evaluated on the last 5.
\end{itemize}

The 13.7\% of Table~\ref{tab:industrial} is in sample; on unseen weeks the same method removes 6.6\% and 7.4\% (Table~\ref{tab:temporal}), which is the expectation a site re-slotting on recent history should plan on. The shortfall is not solver quality: an oracle optimised on the test weeks themselves removes only 12.1\% and 10.2\% there, so demand shifts between the fitting and evaluation windows, and the fitted layouts retain 55\% and 72\% of what the oracle attains. The two splits vary training and evaluation windows together, so the effect of each is not separated, and the stability observed here may not carry to more volatile assortments.

\subsection{Bounded reassignment}\label{sec:reassign}
An established facility cannot absorb a disruptive re-slotting wave \citep{chabot2024reassignment}, so we bound how many products may move. With $s'(p)$ the legacy station of $p$, one constraint
\begin{equation}
\sum_{p \in P} x_{p,\,s'(p)} \ \ge\ |P| - k
\label{eq:reassign}
\end{equation}
limits the relocated products to at most $k$. Nothing else changes and the legacy layout stays feasible for every $k$, so the model has a solution whatever budget the site sets.

The cap also fits the column-generation framework of Section~\ref{sec:cg}. Counting, for each bundle $q$, how many of its products would leave their legacy station,
\[
d_q \;=\; \bigl|\{\, p \in q \,:\, s'(p) \neq s(q) \,\}\bigr|,
\]
where $s(q)$ is the station bundle $q$ is installed at, the cap becomes the single master row $\sum_{q} d_q\, y_q \le k$. Its dual enters pricing as a per-product relocation penalty, so the subproblem proposes a bundle only when the visits it saves outweigh the relocation budget it consumes. We have not run that variant: every bounded-reassignment result below comes from the set-variable formulation, solved for $k \in \{250, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000\}$ under a one-hour limit each, against the legacy layout ($k=0$) and the full re-optimisation (Table~\ref{tab:reassign} and Figure~\ref{fig:ksweep}).

\section{Managerial insights}\label{sec:managerial}
% =====================================================================
The results yield four implications for warehouse operations.

\textbf{(1) Track station visits as a slotting indicator.} Each diversion generates mechanical queueing and handling delay, so stops per order expose congestion that a distance metric hides.

\textbf{(2) Build the labour figure on the out-of-sample reduction.} The reduction a layout shows on the weeks it was fitted to overstates what a site will operate with; the out-of-sample figure of Section~\ref{sec:temporal}, scaled by the site's own per-visit cycle time, is the planning basis. The projection ignores queueing, so it stays a planning figure until a discrete-event study confirms it.

\textbf{(3) Match the tool to the decision, and to the warehouse.} Where stations differ in geometry and speed, as Company~A's do, the set-variable formulation gives the best layout we obtain within capacity; where they are interchangeable the choice shifts with catalogue size (Section~\ref{sec:reliability}). Where minimal disturbance to an engineered balance is the priority, the column generation is the choice, on the evidence of its utilisation dispersion (Table~\ref{tab:industrial}). The clustering heuristic suits a layout needed quickly or often, provided station loads are checked against the site's own tolerance and, on heterogeneous stations, the community bound is selected on a grid (Section~\ref{sec:industrial}).

\textbf{(4) Run the layout as a lifecycle.} A layout optimised on the most recent 8 to 10 weeks retains between half and three quarters of the reduction an oracle could obtain on the following weeks (Section~\ref{sec:temporal}). We did not vary the re-optimisation frequency, so windows of this length are a starting point for a re-slotting cycle, not a validated cadence; bounded reassignment keeps each cycle non-disruptive by moving only as many products as the workforce can handle, every cap of Table~\ref{tab:reassign} staying within capacity and workload.

\section{Conclusions and research perspectives}\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================
We posed the correlated storage location assignment problem for automated pick-and-pass warehouses with station visits as the objective and hard capacity and workload limits, replacing the travel-distance objective that correlated storage models inherit from manual picking. On a real 21,874-product warehouse the model removes 13.7\% of station visits in sample and 6.6 to 7.4\% on weeks it was not fitted to, within every station limit, and a relocation cap lets a site take that gain in stages. The set-variable formulation gives the deepest reduction we obtain and is the form we would deploy; the binary encoding of the same model, run on the same engine, carries 7.8\% more visits at industrial scale while the two are indistinguishable on the synthetic benchmark. We attribute that divergence to the decision representation, though the mechanism is not instrumented, and on this evidence a benchmark of a few thousand products does not settle the choice of representation.

The two other strategies divide the remaining ground. The set-partitioning column generation, driven as a price-and-complete matheuristic, improves the live industrial layout by 5.8\% while disturbing the engineered balance least, and overtakes the reference between 1,000 and 2,000 products on this benchmark. The clustering heuristic is the fast fallback: it returns fewer visits than the reference at 2,000 products in a fraction of the time, and on the industrial site it removes 6.2\% but spends the whole workload tolerance, so it belongs where a layout is needed quickly and not where a ten-hour budget is available.

Four perspectives follow from the limits of this study.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Dual stabilisation} \citep{dumerle1999stabilized, wentges1997weighted}. Section~\ref{sec:cgbound} gives the measured reason for preferring it to a stronger formulation: the master never priced out inside the cap, so the bound is limited by how far the dual iteration got and not by the relaxation's strength, and reaching the value of the full linear master would turn the matheuristic reading into certified gaps.
\item \textbf{Ablation and further baselines.} A fuller account would isolate the drive's operators by ablation and carry an adaptive large neighbourhood search and an open-source constraint-programming model \citep{ropke2006alns, perron2023cpsat} as further baselines, the latter measuring the cost to a practitioner without a commercial licence.
\item \textbf{Product geometry and duplication.} Replacing the unit count of a station's capacity with a per-product footprint $f_p$ in \eqref{eq:cap} would admit three-dimensional packing limits without changing the structure of the programme, and under slack capacity the covering form of \eqref{eq:assign} already admits storing a high-demand product at several stations, which the tight-capacity setting of this study excludes.
\item \textbf{From visits to throughput.} We balance workload, which lowers expected queueing, but do not model the queue. Feeding the optimised layouts into a discrete-event simulation would convert the planning figures of Section~\ref{sec:managerial} into measured throughput and open the way to stochastic demand and real-time routing. What these layouts are worth in throughput rather than stops is the question we leave open.
\end{enumerate}
