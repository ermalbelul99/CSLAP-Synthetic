## 2. Relation to the journal's aims and scope

The paper studies an automated pick-and-pass warehouse as a constrained material-handling system in
which three elements interact: where products are stored, how often each picking station must be
diverted to, and the engineered daily workload each station can absorb. We show that the storage
decision inherited from manual picking — minimise picker travel distance — does not describe this
system at all, and replace it with a model whose objective is the number of station visits an order
incurs, subject to hard per-station slot capacity and workload budgets. The contribution is therefore
a systems-level operations decision with a validated deployment path rather than a purely
methodological result: we solve the model on an operating facility of 21,874 stock-keeping units and
26 stations, measure the reduction on weeks the layout was not fitted to, and bound how many products
may relocate so a live site can take the gain gradually. This places the work squarely in the
journal's operations and logistics remit.

## 3. Statement of contribution (≤100 words — **count before sending**)

> Draft below is **94 words**. Recount if edited; the limit is explicit.

We reformulate the correlated storage location assignment problem for automated pick-and-pass
warehouses, replacing the travel-distance objective inherited from manual picking with the number of
station visits an order incurs, under hard per-station capacity and workload budgets. On an operating
warehouse of 21,874 products and 26 stations the model removes 13.7% of station visits in sample and
6.6–7.4% on held-out weeks, within every station limit, and a relocation cap makes the gain
deployable in stages. Three solution methods and two literature baselines, compared under one
protocol across 29 instances, establish which method delivers that result.
