\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This document is a complete technical post-mortem of a computational
experiment on robust storage location assignment in a pick-and-pass
warehouse. It records what was modelled, what was measured, what failed,
how each failure was diagnosed, and --- most importantly --- which claims
survived adversarial code review, adversarial methodological review, and a
dedicated seed-replication experiment, and which had to be retracted.

The headline modelling result is that the legacy \emph{revealed-peak}
workload contract was \emph{provably} unusable: at sub-maximum quantiles the
station ceilings summed to less than the busiest training day, so two of
three declared parameter settings described an empty feasible set. Replacing
it with a \emph{volume-normalised share band} removes that pathology by
construction and is what first made robust budgets $\Gamma\ge 2$ solvable at
all. The headline empirical result is that budgets $\Gamma\in\{1,2\}$ reduce
out-of-sample contract violations relative to the nominal layout by a margin
exceeding the solver's own run-to-run spread. The headline \emph{negative}
result is that the Bertsimas--\c{S}im coverage mechanism does not explain
why: in the winning layouts the robust row is slack almost everywhere, and
the realised overshoots would have required budgets far outside the declared
range.

Written for a reader who must be able to defend every number in it.
\end{abstract}

\section{The Physical System and What Is Actually Being Optimised}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{The warehouse}

The system is a \textbf{pick-and-pass order-picking line}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item a catalogue of \textbf{2{,}000 SKUs} (stock-keeping units, i.e.\ distinct products);
  \item \textbf{24 picking stations}, each a physical workstation along a conveyor;
  \item customer \textbf{orders are multi-item}: one order requires several distinct products;
  \item a tote representing an order travels along the conveyor and stops at
        \emph{every station holding at least one product in that order}.
\end{itemize}

The decision is the \textbf{storage location assignment}: a binary mapping
$x_{ps}\in\{0,1\}$, with $x_{ps}=1$ iff product $p$ is stored at station $s$,
subject to
\[
  \sum_{s\in S} x_{ps} \;=\; 1 \qquad \forall p \in P,
\]
i.e.\ every product has exactly one home (assumption A5, Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}).

This is the \textbf{Correlated Storage Location Assignment Problem (CSLAP)}.
The word \emph{correlated} is load-bearing: the objective depends on which
products \emph{co-occur} within orders, not on individual product popularity.
Two slow-moving SKUs that always appear together should share a station; two
fast movers that never co-occur should not.

\subsection{The objective: station visits, not travel distance}

\begin{equation}
  V \;=\; \sum_{o\in O}\bigl|\{\, s\in S \;:\; \exists\, p\in o \text{ with } x_{ps}=1 \,\}\bigr|
  \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}

$V$ counts, summed over all orders $o$, the number of \emph{distinct} stations
that order must visit.

\paragraph{Why not picker travel distance?}
In a classical picker-to-parts warehouse the cost driver is the walking
distance of the picker, and the literature minimises tour length. In a
pick-and-pass conveyor system the picker does not walk --- the tote comes to
them. The cost driver is structurally different:

\begin{itemize}
  \item each station visit costs a \emph{stop}, a \emph{buffer slot}, and \emph{handling time};
  \item buffers between stations are finite;
  \item if a station's buffer fills, the tote cannot enter and waits \emph{on the main line};
  \item a tote waiting on the main line \emph{blocks every tote behind it}, irrespective of destination.
\end{itemize}

A station visit is therefore not a local cost but a \emph{systemic congestion
risk}. This is why $V$ is the correct objective, and why the workload
constraint below is a hard operational matter rather than a soft preference.

Two quantities are reported throughout:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Symbol & Meaning \\
\midrule
$\vtr$ & visits generated by a layout on the \textbf{training} order set (in-sample) \\
$\vte$ & visits generated by the \emph{same} layout on the \textbf{future test} order set \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$\vte$ is the scientifically meaningful quantity. $\vtr$ is what the optimiser
sees; $\vte$ is what the warehouse would actually have paid. A layout that
minimises $\vtr$ but degrades on $\vte$ has \textbf{overfitted to history} ---
which is the entire reason this study exists.

For reference, the site's own layout produced $\vte = 210{,}364$ visits pooled
across the four test windows.

\subsection{The operational constraint: daily pick-lines per station}

A \textbf{pick line} is one (order, product) pick event. Let
\[
  W_s(d) \;=\; \text{number of pick-lines executed at station } s \text{ on day } d .
\]

Each station has bounded daily throughput. Exceeding it produces one of three
real consequences:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{overtime} --- the labour budget is breached (cost);
  \item \textbf{spillover} --- work is pushed to the next day (service-level breach);
  \item \textbf{blocking} --- the buffer saturates and the main line stalls
        (throughput collapse for the \emph{entire} system).
\end{enumerate}

Critically, \textbf{the stations are physically heterogeneous}: static shelving,
dynamic/flow racks, and pallet positions, spanning roughly a
\textbf{1{,}700$\times$ range in speed}. This is the single most important
physical fact in the study.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The structural tension.} A naive ``balance the load equally''
constraint is \emph{wrong}, because a small station legitimately should carry
less. Meanwhile an unconstrained visit-minimiser wants to pile the
best-sellers onto the largest station, since that merges orders into fewer
stations --- which is exactly what overloads it. \textbf{The objective pulls
towards concentration; the constraint pushes towards dispersion.} Everything
downstream is about specifying that constraint correctly.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Assumptions fixed in advance}
\label{sec:assumptions}

These were declared before any solving and never silently changed:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cp{0.82\linewidth}}
\toprule
ID & Assumption \\
\midrule
A1 & The workload constraint binds \textbf{per DAY}, not per aggregated window. \\
A2 & The station ceiling is \textbf{revealed from history}, not engineered from a spec sheet. \\
A3 & Future demand lies inside the observed envelope; larger surges are out of scope. \\
A4 & The robust budget $\Gamma$ insures the \textbf{idiosyncratic} component ($\approx$60\%); the \textbf{common} component ($\approx$40\%) is absorbed structurally or priced by $\beta$. \\
A5 & Closed universe: fixed catalogue, every product has a home, no new SKUs. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A1 is not cosmetic. Aggregating over a week lets a Monday spike and a Friday
trough cancel, rendering the constraint vacuous --- precisely the failure mode
that makes a nominally ``feasible'' layout blow up in operation.

\section{Mathematical Formulations: From a Broken Contract to the Share Band}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{The legacy contract: revealed-peak fixed ceilings}

The original formulation assigned each station an absolute ceiling $T_s$ in
lines/day, derived as a $q$-quantile of that station's realised daily load
\emph{under the currently operating layout}, with $q$ swept over
$\{\max, p_{95}, p_{90}\}$:

\begin{equation}
  W_s(d) \;\le\; T_s,
  \qquad
  T_s \;=\; Q_q\bigl(\{W_s^{\mathrm{inc}}(d)\}_{d\in\mathrm{train}}\bigr).
  \label{eq:legacy}
\end{equation}

This failed in three independent ways. Diagnosing them motivated everything else.

\subsubsection*{Failure 1 --- circularity}

$T_s$ is measured from the incumbent layout's own behaviour. A uniformity
diagnostic was run across five candidate contract forms, measuring for each the
\textbf{coefficient of variation} of the parameter each station requires:
\[
  \mathrm{cv} \;=\;
  \frac{\text{sd across stations of the required parameter}}
       {\text{mean across stations}} .
\]
A cv near $0$ means every station needs the \emph{same} parameter value. Here
that is not a virtue but a symptom: the contract is a tautology.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcl}
\toprule
Contract form & cv & Verdict \\
\midrule
own-max (revealed peak)                 & \textbf{0.00} & circular: fits itself by construction \\
own-mean $+\,z\cdot$own-$\sigma$ (absolute) & 0.22 & A4 fails: absorbs no common drift \\
\textbf{share-mean $+\,z\cdot$share-$\sigma$} & \textbf{0.30} & \textbf{chosen} \\
share $\times$ slack                    & 0.70 & unstable \\
share $+\,z\sqrt{\cdot}$                & 0.99 & unstable \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection*{Failure 2 --- provable counting infeasibility at $q<\max$}

This is a \textbf{solver-free proof}, which is why it is more trustworthy than
any solver output. Let $\mathcal{L}(d)=\sum_s W_s(d)$ be the warehouse-wide
lines on day $d$. Every line must be executed somewhere, so a necessary
condition for a feasible layout on day $d$ is
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{s\in S} T_s \;\ge\; \mathcal{L}(d) \qquad \forall d .
  \label{eq:counting}
\end{equation}

Measured:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Fold & $\sum_s T_s$ at $q_{p95}$ / $q_{p90}$ & busiest day $\max_d \mathcal{L}(d)$ & Verdict \\
\midrule
0     & 18{,}841 / 17{,}890 & \textbf{19{,}692} & infeasible \\
1--3  & $\approx$20{,}200 / $\approx$18{,}700 & \textbf{24{,}649} & infeasible \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{A1 (per-day binding) and A2's sub-max quantile sweep are
mathematically incompatible.} No solver was needed, and no solver could have
rescued it: two of the three declared $q$ values described an empty feasible
set.
\end{keybox}

\subsubsection*{Failure 3 --- zero slack for the robust budget}

Define the \textbf{aggregate slack} available to protection and the
\textbf{protection requirement} at budget $\Gamma$:
\[
  \text{slack} \;=\; \sum_s T_s - \max_d \mathcal{L}(d),
  \qquad
  \text{prot}(\Gamma) \;=\; \sum_s \ \sum_{i\le\Gamma} \hat L_{(i),s},
\]
where $\hat L_{(i),s}$ are the deviations of products assigned to $s$, sorted
descending.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccl}
\toprule
Fold & slack & $\Gamma=1$ needs & $\Gamma=2$ needs & Outcome \\
\midrule
0    & 1{,}397 & 889 \yes & 1{,}486 \no & $\Gamma\le1$ only \\
1--3 & 798     & $\approx$890 \no & --- & $\Gamma=0$ only \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

This \emph{exactly} reproduced which cells solved and which did not, fold by
fold.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{A recorded negative result.} Both a reviewer and I believed the missing
dual warm start caused the $\Gamma\ge2$ failures. The dual seeding fix was
implemented --- and $\Gamma\ge2$ \emph{still} failed. The budget arithmetic was
the cause, not the solver. The fix was correct and necessary; the hypothesis
about what it would buy was wrong. A rejected hypothesis that cost real work is
worth recording.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{The redesigned contract: the volume-normalised share band}

\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  W_s(d) \;\le\; \bigl(\mu_s + z\,\sigma_s\bigr)\cdot L(d)
  \;}
  \label{eq:shareband}
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{clc}
\toprule
Term & Definition & Units \\
\midrule
$W_s(d)$  & pick-lines executed at station $s$ on day $d$ & lines \\
$L(d)$    & \textbf{total} warehouse-wide pick-lines on day $d$ & lines \\
$\mu_s$   & mean over \textbf{training} days of station $s$'s \emph{share} of the day, under the incumbent & --- \\
$\sigma_s$& sample sd (ddof$=1$) of that same share over training days & --- \\
$z$       & tolerance multiplier, in units of the station's own share variability & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

with
\[
  \mathrm{share}_s(d) = \frac{W_s(d)}{L(d)},
  \qquad
  \mu_s = \frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{d\in D}\mathrm{share}_s(d),
  \qquad
  \sigma_s^2 = \frac{1}{|D|-1}\sum_{d\in D}\bigl(\mathrm{share}_s(d)-\mu_s\bigr)^2 .
\]

\subsubsection*{Why the normalisation is the entire point}

Divide both sides of \eqref{eq:shareband} by $L(d)$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{share}_s(d) \;\le\; \mu_s + z\,\sigma_s .
  \label{eq:normalised}
\end{equation}

\textbf{The right-hand side no longer contains $L(d)$.} Decompose demand
multiplicatively:
\[
  W_s(d) \;=\; \underbrace{L(d)}_{\text{common}} \;\cdot\;
               \underbrace{\mathrm{share}_s(d)}_{\text{idiosyncratic}} .
\]

A \textbf{common shock} --- a 30\% busier week with every product up
proportionally --- multiplies $L(d)$ and cancels identically on both sides. It
cannot break this constraint. What \emph{can} break it is a change in
\emph{composition}: one station's share of the day drifting upward.

Contrast with the absolute ceiling $W_s(d)\le T_s$: there a 30\% volume surge
consumes 30\% more of a fixed ceiling \emph{regardless of composition}, so
$\Gamma$ would have to insure common risk as well.

\begin{keybox}
This is precisely why assumption A4 was unsatisfiable under the legacy contract
and is satisfiable here. The normalisation is not a convenience --- it is what
makes the budget's job well-posed.
\end{keybox}

\subsubsection*{Verified structural identities}

Gated (Z2, Z3) and holding exactly:
\begin{align}
  \sum_s \mathrm{share}_s(d) &= 1 \quad \forall d
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  \sum_s \mu_s = 1
  \quad(\text{measured: } 1.000000000000), \label{eq:sumshare}\\[2pt]
  \sum_s \bigl(\mu_s + z\sigma_s\bigr) &= 1 + z\sum_s \sigma_s \;>\; 1 .
  \label{eq:permission}
\end{align}

Identity \eqref{eq:permission} is load-bearing: \textbf{total permission always
strictly exceeds 100\% of the day}, so the aggregate counting infeasibility
\eqref{eq:counting} that killed the legacy contract is impossible by
construction. With $\sum_s\sigma_s \approx 0.1209$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
$z$ & total permission & aggregate slack \\
\midrule
3 & 1.363$\times$ & 36.3\% of the day \\
4 & 1.483$\times$ & 48.3\% \\
6 & 1.725$\times$ & 72.5\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

That surplus is the budget $\Gamma$ has to spend.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The Bertsimas--\c{S}im robust model}{The Bertsimas-Sim robust model}}

\subsubsection*{Philosophy}

Classical \textbf{worst-case (box) robustness} assumes every uncertain
parameter simultaneously attains its worst value. For 2{,}000 products this is
absurdly conservative and yields an unusable solution.

\textbf{Bertsimas--\c{S}im budgeted uncertainty} parameterises the
conservatism. With each product's daily line count in
$[\bar L_p - \hat L_p,\ \bar L_p + \hat L_p]$, the uncertainty set is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{U}_\Gamma \;=\;
  \Bigl\{\, L \;:\; L_p = \bar L_p + \eta_p \hat L_p,\;\;
  |\eta_p|\le 1,\;\; \sum_p |\eta_p| \le \Gamma \,\Bigr\}.
  \label{eq:uset}
\end{equation}

$\Gamma$ is the \textbf{budget of uncertainty}: at most $\Gamma$ products may
deviate adversely at once. $\Gamma=0$ recovers the nominal deterministic model;
$\Gamma=|P|$ recovers full box robustness. In between lies a tunable dial with
a probabilistic violation guarantee that degrades gracefully in $\Gamma$.

The robust workload constraint at station $s$ on day $d$ becomes
\begin{equation}
  \sum_p \bar L_p x_{ps}
  \;+\;
  \underbrace{\max_{S\subseteq P,\ |S|\le\Gamma}\ \sum_{p\in S}\hat L_p x_{ps}}_{\text{protection term}}
  \;\le\; \mathrm{RHS}_s(d).
  \label{eq:robustraw}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Dualisation}

The protection term is a \emph{maximum inside a $\le$ constraint} ---
non-linear as written. The standard resolution is LP duality. Relax the
selection to continuous $u_p\in[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
  \max_u \ \sum_p \hat L_p x_{ps} u_p
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad
  \sum_p u_p \le \Gamma, \quad 0\le u_p \le 1 .
  \label{eq:innerlp}
\end{equation}

This relaxation is \textbf{exact}: the constraint matrix is a unit-weight
knapsack (an interval matrix, totally unimodular), so an integral optimum
always exists. Its \textbf{LP dual}, with $\theta_s$ dual to the budget row and
$\mu_{ps}$ dual to $u_p\le1$, is
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\theta,\mu} \ \Gamma\theta_s + \sum_p \mu_{ps}
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad
  \theta_s + \mu_{ps} \ge \hat L_p x_{ps}, \quad \theta_s,\mu_{ps}\ge 0 .
  \label{eq:dual}
\end{equation}

By \textbf{strong duality} the two optima coincide. Substituting the $\min$
form into a $\le$ constraint permits dropping the minimisation (any
dual-feasible point satisfying the constraint suffices, and the optimiser
drives it down):

\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \sum_p \bar L_p x_{ps} + \Gamma\theta_s + \sum_p \mu_{ps} \;\le\; \mathrm{RHS}_s(d),
  \qquad
  \theta_s + \mu_{ps} \;\ge\; a_{ps}\,x_{ps}
  \;}
  \label{eq:bsfinal}
\end{equation}

Everything is now linear. In the share-band implementation the per-unit
deviation is $a_{ps}=\hat L_p$, identical across stations, because the band is
expressed in \emph{lines} and the speed division required by the legacy
time-based contract has vanished --- a unit-consistency property the code
review verified independently.

\subsubsection*{Why tight dual bounding boxes mattered}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Definition.} A \emph{bounding box} is the a-priori interval within
which the solver is allowed to search for each variable.
\end{keybox}

The original implementation declared $\theta_s,\mu_{ps}\in[0,10^9]$. Two
analytic facts shrink this enormously:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\theta_s \le a_{\max}(s) := \max_p a_{ps}$. If $\theta_s$ exceeded the
        largest coefficient, all $\mu_{ps}=0$ and the dual objective
        $\Gamma\theta_s$ would strictly exceed the true maximum --- never optimal.
  \item $\mu_{ps} \le a_{ps}$, since $\mu_{ps}\ge a_{ps}x_{ps}-\theta_s$ with
        $x\le1$ and $\theta\ge0$.
\end{itemize}

There are $2000\times24 = 48{,}000$ such variables. Shrinking each from
$[0,10^9]$ to its analytic range removes roughly \textbf{nine orders of
magnitude of dead search space per variable}. For a local-search solver, which
samples moves within the declared domain, this is the difference between a
search that explores and one that flails.

\subsubsection*{Dual seeding at the warm start}

For a \emph{fixed} assignment the optimal duals have a closed form. Let
$a_{(1)}\ge a_{(2)}\ge\cdots$ be the deviations of products assigned to $s$,
sorted descending. Then
\begin{equation}
  \theta_s^\star = a_{(\Gamma)},
  \qquad
  \mu_{ps}^\star = \bigl(a_{ps}-\theta_s^\star\bigr)^{+} .
  \label{eq:dualseed}
\end{equation}

Verification that this reproduces the true maximum:
\[
  \Gamma\theta^\star + \sum_p \mu^\star_{ps}
  \;=\;
  \Gamma a_{(\Gamma)} + \sum_{i=1}^{\Gamma}\bigl(a_{(i)}-a_{(\Gamma)}\bigr)
  \;=\;
  \sum_{i=1}^{\Gamma} a_{(i)} \qquad\checkmark
\]

Seeding $x$ \emph{without} the duals leaves the warm start dual-infeasible: the
solver receives a point violating $\theta_s+\mu_{ps}\ge a_{ps}x_{ps}$ and must
first repair it, destroying the value of the warm start.

\section{Complete Parameter Glossary}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$z\in\{3,4,6\}$}{z in {3,4,6}} --- tolerance / bandwidth multiplier}

\textbf{Definition.} The number of station-share standard deviations of
headroom granted above the station's mean share.

\textbf{Role.} $z$ \emph{is the contract}. It defines what counts as an
acceptable day. It is \emph{not} a treatment and never a tuning knob.

\textbf{Bounded above and below.} As $z\to\infty$ the constraint becomes
vacuous and the model reverts to the degenerate ``pile every best-seller onto
the biggest station'' solution the constraint exists to prevent. As $z\to0$ it
demands a balance the site has never achieved.

\textbf{Empirical anchors (corrected; see Section~\ref{sec:folds}).}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
$z$ & days fully clean (pre-registered, 179 nested slots) & days fully clean (\textbf{51 distinct days}) \\
\midrule
2.0 & 49.7\% & 51.0\% \\
2.7 & 74.3\% & 70.6\% \\
3.0 & 80.4\% & 80.4\% \\
4.0 & 94.4\% & \textbf{94.1\%} \\
6.0 & 99.4\% & \textbf{98.0\%} \\
8.0 & 100.0\% & 100.0\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Bounds.} The incumbent needs $z=6.07$ to be clean on \emph{every}
training day (upper bound: above it we would license a layout more imbalanced
than the site has ever operated). The median station needs $z\approx2.86$
(lower bound).

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Reported as measured coverage, never as a sigma level.} The share
distribution has \emph{fatter tails than Gaussian}: at $z=3$ a normal law
predicts 99.865\% of station-days clean, but 99.07\% is observed. Calling
$z=3$ ``three sigma'' would import a tail probability the data does not support.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\Gamma\in\{0,1,2,4\}$}{Gamma} --- Bertsimas--Sim budget}

\textbf{Definition.} The maximum number of products assumed to deviate
adversely \emph{simultaneously} at a given station.

\textbf{Role.} $\Gamma$ \emph{is the treatment}. Comparisons across $\Gamma$
are valid \textbf{only within a fixed $z$} (gate Z13). Comparing $\Gamma=4$ at
$z=6$ against $\Gamma=0$ at $z=3$ changes contract and treatment together, and
would credit a loosened contract to the robust budget.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\beta\in\{1.02,1.05\}$}{beta} --- deterministic band tightening}

\textbf{Definition.} A uniform contraction of the allowance:
\begin{equation}
  W_s(d) \;\le\; \frac{\mu_s + z\sigma_s}{\beta}\cdot L(d).
  \label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Role.} The \textbf{competitor arm}. $\beta$ buys safety by shrinking
everyone's allowance uniformly and blindly; $\Gamma$ buys it by targeting the
products whose volatility actually threatens each station. Comparing them asks:
\emph{is the Bertsimas--\c{S}im machinery earning its complexity, or would a
crude uniform haircut do as well?}

\textbf{Historical note.} $\beta$ was a silent no-op for one development phase:
the per-day right-hand side overrode the tightened capacity, so $\beta$
duplicated $\Gamma=0$ (measured $-0.01\%$ and $-0.19\%$ --- noise). Caught,
guarded, then fixed.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\hat L_p$}{lhat} --- per-product daily volatility}

\textbf{Definition.}
$\hat L_p = \min\bigl(\sigma_p^{\mathrm{daily}},\ \bar L_p\bigr)$,
where $\sigma_p^{\mathrm{daily}}$ is the standard deviation of product $p$'s
daily line count over training days and $\bar L_p$ its mean.

\textbf{Why the cap.} Pick-line counts are non-negative. Without
$\hat L_p\le\bar L_p$ the interval $[\bar L_p-\hat L_p,\ \bar L_p+\hat L_p]$
would extend below zero --- physically meaningless, and it silently inflates
the protection term.

\textbf{Role.} $\hat L_p$ is the \emph{price} of protecting product $p$.
High-volatility SKUs are expensive to insure; stable ones are cheap. This is
what makes $\Gamma$ selective rather than uniform.

\subsection{Price of Robustness and out-of-sample gain}

\begin{align}
  \PoR &= \frac{\vtr(\Gamma)-\vtr(0)}{\vtr(0)}
  &&\text{in-sample visits given up to buy protection} \label{eq:por}\\[3pt]
  G    &= \frac{\vte(0)-\vte(\Gamma)}{\vte(0)}
  &&\text{visits recovered on future data} \label{eq:gain}
\end{align}

$\PoR \ge 0$ always holds for \emph{exact} optima, since a smaller feasible set
cannot have a better optimum. $G>0$ would mean protection paid for itself.

\textbf{What was measured:} $\PoR = +7\%$ to $+15\%$; $G$ \textbf{negative
throughout}. Robustness costs visits and does not repay them \emph{in visits}.
It repays in \emph{constraint reliability} --- a different currency, whose
operational meaning Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} examines critically.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\gbind$}{gamma-bind} --- where protection starts to bite}

\textbf{Definition.} For a given layout and station, the smallest budget at
which the robust row would actually cut off that layout on training data.
Operationally: sort the station's own $\hat L_p$ descending, accumulate, and
find the first index at which the cumulative sum exceeds the station's
worst-day headroom.

\textbf{Interpretation.} Below $\gbind$, raising $\Gamma$ changes nothing at
that station --- the constraint is slack. At or above it, the optimiser is
forced to rebalance.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\gcover$}{gamma-cover} --- what the future asked for}

\textbf{Definition.} The smallest budget whose protection would have absorbed
the station's \emph{realised test-window overshoot}; zero when the station
never overshot.

\textbf{Interpretation.} $\gbind$ is about the model; $\gcover$ is about
reality. Reporting both permits the statement \emph{``protection is useful at
these stations, for this reason''} rather than the much weaker \emph{``arm A
beat arm B''}.

\subsection{Incumbent layout}

\textbf{Definition.} The assignment the site actually operates. It plays three
distinct roles, and conflating them is a trap:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{statistical basis} --- $\mu_s,\sigma_s$ are fitted from \emph{its} behaviour;
  \item \textbf{warm start} --- the solver's initial point;
  \item \textbf{business baseline} --- what any proposed layout must beat.
\end{enumerate}

Role 1 raises a legitimate objection: does fitting the band to the incumbent
rig the comparison in its favour?

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Tested directly: no.} The incumbent violates its own fitted band on
\textbf{35 / 10 / 1} of 179 training slots at $z=3/4/6$, with worst-day ratios
up to \textbf{1.767}. The band is not a tautology for the layout it was fitted
on, because the share distribution's tails exceed $\mu+z\sigma$.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Two solver paradigms}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.24\linewidth}p{0.33\linewidth}p{0.33\linewidth}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{CPLEX / HiGHS (exact MIP)} & \textbf{Hexaly (local search)} \\
\midrule
method & branch-and-bound with cutting planes over an LP relaxation & metaheuristic neighbourhood search over feasible points \\
returns & primal solution \emph{and} dual bound & primal solution only \\
proves optimality & yes (gap $\to0$) & \textbf{no} \\
proves infeasibility & yes (exhausted tree) & \textbf{no} \\
scaling & degrades sharply with model size & handles $10^5$ variables routinely \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Hexaly was run at a \textbf{600\,s limit per arm}. Two consequences must be
stated plainly:

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Every arm hit its time limit.} Not one terminated on an optimality
criterion. That is inherent to the method, not a failure --- but it means
\emph{no arm in this study is claimed optimal}, and every reported $\PoR$ is a
difference between two heuristic incumbents.

\textbf{The dual bounds are effectively vacuous.} Across the 70 feasible rows,
$\text{bound}/\text{obj}$ ranges $0.000$--$0.233$ (median $0.073$); one bound is
literally zero. \textbf{No arm is certified within 77\% of optimal.}
\end{keybox}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\texttt{INFEASIBLE} vs \texttt{INCONSISTENT}}{INFEASIBLE vs INCONSISTENT}}

Hexaly returns two different negative statuses, and collapsing them would be
scientific misconduct:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{0.48\linewidth}l}
\toprule
Status & Meaning & Encoding \\
\midrule
\texttt{INCONSISTENT} & presolve derived a \textbf{contradiction} --- proven empty feasible set & bound $=+\infty$, proven \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Gate Z16 enforces that no row claims a proven infeasibility, because a
local-search solver cannot produce one. The two arms that failed in the final
grid ($z=3$, $\Gamma=4$, folds 2 and 3) are recorded as \textbf{undetermined},
never as infeasible.

\begin{keybox}
A ``MODEL INFEASIBLE (proven)'' verdict is a legitimate scientific result. A
\emph{claimed} one that the method cannot support is a fabrication.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Temporal validation and the nested-fold correction}
\label{sec:folds}

\textbf{Rolling-origin expanding-window cross-validation.} Unlike $k$-fold CV,
which shuffles and would leak future into past, temporal validation always
trains on a prefix and tests on the immediately following block. Each fold's
training window \emph{expands} to include everything before its test window.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
\toprule
Fold & train days & training window & test days & test window \\
\midrule
0 & 38 & 2021-09-01 $\to$ 10-22 & 5 & 10-25 $\to$ 10-29 \\
1 & 43 & 2021-09-01 $\to$ 10-29 & 4 & 11-02 $\to$ 11-05 \\
2 & 47 & 2021-09-01 $\to$ 11-05 & 4 & 11-08 $\to$ 11-12 \\
3 & 51 & 2021-09-01 $\to$ 11-12 & 5 & 11-15 $\to$ 11-19 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection*{The correction that matters}

The pre-registration stated ``179 training days''. That is $38+43+47+51$ --- a
\textbf{sum over nested sets}. It was verified that fold $f$'s training set is a
strict superset of fold $f-1$'s, all beginning 2021-09-01. Therefore
\begin{equation}
  \text{distinct training days}
  \;=\; \Bigl|\textstyle\bigcup_f D_f^{\mathrm{train}}\Bigr|
  \;=\; \mathbf{51}, \qquad\text{not } 179 .
  \label{eq:distinct}
\end{equation}

\textbf{The earliest 38 days are counted four times.} The whole record is
\textbf{56 days} ($\approx$11 weeks, one site).

This inflated the pre-registered coverage anchors, and worst exactly where it
mattered: $z=6$ was justified as ``matches demonstrated status quo, 99.4\%''. On
distinct days it is \textbf{98.0\%} --- the incumbent leaves one of 51 days
unclean.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Clopper--Pearson interval.} The \emph{exact} binomial confidence
interval, obtained by inverting the binomial test rather than relying on a
normal approximation --- essential at these tiny counts. On $1/51$ it admits a
true unclean rate up to roughly 10\%. The $z$-window is a point estimate from
51 days with no interval, and its upper bound $z\le6.07$ is a \emph{maximum set
by a single day}.
\end{keybox}

This is now corrected in the pre-registration, with the original kept verbatim,
and pinned by gate Z19 so it cannot drift back.

\section{The Gate Ledger and Every Engineering Failure}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{What a gate ledger is}

Each gate is a claim paired with an \textbf{executable oracle}, written
\emph{before} the data exists. A gate is \emph{met} only if its process exits 0
\textbf{and} its declared \texttt{EXPECT:} token appears in the output. The
runner writes evidence back into the ledger with a hash and a timing.

Crucially, every gate is \textbf{mutation-tested}: fed a fixture it should
accept, then the same fixture carrying exactly the defect it exists to catch,
which it must reject.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{A gate that has only ever passed is not evidence} --- you have not shown
that it \emph{can} fail.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{The 120\,s pilot failure --- a pilot doing its job}

At a reduced 120\,s limit (a cost-saving deviation, \emph{not} the
pre-registered 600\,s), $\Gamma=4$ returned no feasible layout and gate Z10
rejected the cell at 5 of 6 arms. \textbf{The assertion was not weakened.}
Three independent diagnostics followed:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Aggregate budget.} $\Gamma=4$ needs \textbf{2{,}315 lines};
        the quietest fold-0 day supplies \textbf{2{,}762}. It fits.
  \item \textbf{Proximity.} The nearest solved layout ($\Gamma=2$) missed
        $\Gamma=4$ feasibility by \textbf{2 station-days / 60 lines} out of
        550{,}777 training lines.
  \item \textbf{Direct test.} Re-solved at the pre-registered 600\,s: feasible,
        objective $29{,}704$, $0/38$ days breached.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Verdict: time-limit artefact, not infeasibility.} Returning to 600\,s
was not tuning --- 600\,s was pre-registered; 120\,s was the deviation.

\subsubsection*{An unexpected bonus: the monotone feasibility ladder}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Layout & $\Gamma=0$ & $\Gamma=1$ & $\Gamma=2$ & $\Gamma=4$ \\
\midrule
incumbent & \no\ (8/38) & \no & \no & \no \\
$g0$      & \yes & \no & \no & \no \\
$g1$      & \yes & \yes & \no & \no \\
$g2$      & \yes & \yes & \yes & \no \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Each layout $g_\Gamma$ is feasible at exactly its own budget and breaks at the
next --- strong evidence that the robust row is enforced as specified.

\subsection{The Z7 defect --- the busiest day is the wrong day}

Gate Z7 asserted that $\Gamma=4$'s protection fits inside available slack, and
measured slack on the \textbf{busiest} training day. That is wrong:

\begin{itemize}
  \item protection $\sum_s\sum_{i\le\Gamma}a_{(i)}$ depends on $\hat L_p$ --- a
        \textbf{constant number of lines}, independent of the day;
  \item slack $z\bigl(\sum_s\sigma_s\bigr)L(d)$ \textbf{scales with the day's volume}.
\end{itemize}

Therefore slack is \textbf{smallest on the quietest day}. Checking the busiest
day overstates affordability by the max/min volume ratio, which is
\textbf{2.58$\times$} on these folds.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
 & old (busiest day) & corrected (quietest day) \\
\midrule
tightest cell margin & $\approx3\times$ & \textbf{1.08$\times$} (fold 3, $z=3$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{keybox}
That 1.08$\times$ margin \textbf{predicted exactly where the final grid would
fail}: the two arms that did not solve were $z=3$, $\Gamma=4$, folds 2 and 3. A
corrected gate earned its keep by forecasting the failure.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Mutation testing and the sandboxing bug --- twice}

\textbf{Mutation testing} deliberately injects a defect into a fixture and
requires the test to fail. It answers \emph{``does this check discriminate?''}
rather than \emph{``did this check pass?''}

\paragraph{Incident 1.} The gate-mutation script built its fixture at the real
results directory and deleted the tree on cleanup. That path was empty when the
script was written; by the time of the grid it held the committed pilot.
Running the suite \textbf{destroyed committed evidence}. Recovered from git.

\paragraph{Incident 2 --- worse.} After gate Z10 was retargeted from the 120\,s
pilot to the 600\,s one, the Z10 mutation script kept building at the \emph{old}
path. It therefore mutated one directory while the gate read another ---
\textbf{every mutation ``passed'' while proving nothing} --- and cleanup again
deleted real results. Recovered from git.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The second incident is the more instructive.} Z10 read green for a full
cycle while its validation was vacuous. \emph{A gate is only as good as the
mutation test that validates it, and a mutation test pointed at the wrong
directory is worse than none --- it manufactures false confidence.}
\end{keybox}

\paragraph{Structural fix.} An environment hook
(\texttt{CSLAP\_ZDIR\_SANDBOX}) redirects the gate module's root;
\texttt{checks\_z} prints a loud \texttt{*** SANDBOX ***} banner whenever it is
set, so sandboxed output cannot be mistaken for evidence. The Z10 mutation
script now \emph{imports the gate module and reads the fixture path from it},
then asserts that path lies inside the sandbox before deleting anything. The
fixture cannot drift from the gate, and cannot touch real results.

This belongs to the same failure family as an earlier incident in which a
\LaTeX\ \texttt{*.log} ignore rule silently swallowed 17 solver console logs,
which \texttt{git stash --include-untracked} then skipped, and a worktree
deletion destroyed.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Uncommitted evidence is not evidence.} In both mutation incidents, the
only reason nothing was lost permanently is that each phase had been committed
first.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Gate Z9 and the \texorpdfstring{$\beta$}{beta}-inversion}

On the final grid, \textbf{6 of 24} $\beta$ arms returned objectives
\emph{below} their $\Gamma=0$ baseline (worst $-1.79\%$). For exact optima this
is impossible: $\beta$'s feasible set is a strict subset of $\Gamma=0$'s, so
$\mathrm{opt}_\beta \ge \mathrm{opt}_{\Gamma=0}$.

\textbf{Both explanations were tested rather than assumed.} All 24 $\beta$
layouts satisfy the \emph{tightened} band on every training day and differ from
$\Gamma=0$ by $\approx$1{,}700 of 2{,}000 products. \textbf{$\beta$ is enforced
everywhere.}

The inversion is therefore a \textbf{suboptimality certificate}: a $\beta$
layout is feasible for the tighter band, hence also feasible for $\Gamma=0$'s
looser band. A lower objective at a feasible point of $\Gamma=0$'s problem
\emph{proves} that $\Gamma=0$'s returned incumbent was suboptimal by at least
that margin.

\subsubsection*{Two corrections to my own language}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item I wrote ``suboptimality \emph{bounded at} 1.79\%''. That inverts the
        inequality. An inversion is a \textbf{lower} bound on solver error:
        error $\ge 1.79\%$. The only \emph{upper} bound available is the dual,
        and it is vacuous.
  \item I applied the resulting noise floor \textbf{asymmetrically} --- ruling
        $\beta$'s $+0.4$--$1.2\%$ indistinguishable while reporting
        $\Gamma=2$'s $-1.34\%$ saving at $z=3$ as a win. Both sit under the
        same floor. Now stated symmetrically, and the $z=3$ visit claim is
        retracted.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Z9 was restructured, not loosened.} The original assertion tested a
property of \emph{exact optima} against a \emph{local-search} solver --- invalid
by construction. The replacement checks what ``$\beta$ is binding'' actually
means and holds regardless of solver quality: the returned layout must satisfy
the tightened band on every training day, and must differ from $\Gamma=0$. Both
mutation-tested. This is the one assertion changed after seeing data, and it is
flagged as such in the ledger.

\section{The Adversarial Review and Statistical Audit}
% =====================================================================

Two hostile reviews were commissioned: one on code, one on method. The code
review \emph{recomputed} rather than read, and independently confirmed the core
is sound --- no leakage ($\mu,\sigma$ match a train-only fit to $10^{-12}$ and
differ from a train+test fit on every fold), all arms scored against the same
$\beta=1$ band, units consistent, $\gbind/\gcover$ correct with zero
mismatches, every committed metric reproducible. The methodological review was
far more damaging.

\subsection{Statistical power is capped by design}

\textbf{The unit-of-analysis problem.} There are 18 test days, but they form
\textbf{4 consecutive weeks}, one per fold. Weekday demand is strongly
autocorrelated; 18 days are not 18 independent observations. The honest unit is
the \textbf{week}: $n=4$.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Sign test.} The non-parametric test for paired data using only the
\emph{direction} of each difference. Under $H_0$ each pair is equally likely to
go either way, so the count of positive differences is
$\mathrm{Binomial}(n,\tfrac12)$. With $n=4$ all in one direction,
\[
  p \;=\; 2\times\left(\tfrac12\right)^{4} \;=\; 0.125 .
\]
\textbf{No comparison in this study can reach $p<0.05$ on weekly units. It is
arithmetically impossible.}
\end{keybox}

This should have appeared in the pre-registration; it now does.

\subsection{Finer-grained paired tests}

\textbf{McNemar's test.} The paired test for binary outcomes. Given $n_{01}$
cases where A fails and B does not, and $n_{10}$ the reverse, concordant pairs
carry no information; inference rests on the \textbf{discordant} pairs, testing
whether they split evenly. The exact binomial form was used (appropriate at
these counts, unlike the $\chi^2$ approximation).

On 432 station-days ($18\times24$):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Comparison & $z=3$ & $z=4$ & $z=6$ \\
\midrule
$\Gamma=0$ vs $\Gamma=1$        & $p=0.019$  & $0.035$  & $0.022$ \\
$\Gamma=0$ vs $\Gamma=2$        & $0.0042$   & $0.0034$ & $0.012$ \\
$\Gamma=0$ vs $\beta{=}1.05$    & $0.52$     & $0.12$   & $0.15$  \\
\textbf{incumbent vs $\Gamma=2$}& \textbf{0.51} & \textbf{0.63} & \textbf{1.00} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Three readings:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\Gamma$ versus nominal is nominally significant everywhere;
  \item $\beta$ versus nominal is \textbf{not} significant anywhere --- its
        apparent improvement is not distinguishable from noise;
  \item \textbf{incumbent versus $\Gamma=2$ is a statistical tie at every $z$},
        which directly refutes the earlier ``dominates the incumbent'' claim.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Multiplicity.} With 14 comparisons, \textbf{Bonferroni}
($\alpha/m = 0.0036$) or the uniformly more powerful \textbf{Holm step-down}
procedure (sort $p$ ascending, compare $p_{(i)}$ against $\alpha/(m-i+1)$, stop
at the first failure) leave \textbf{only 1 of 14 surviving}. Both control the
family-wise error rate --- the probability of \emph{any} false rejection.

\paragraph{Why the $\Gamma$ effect is still credible.} Not from $p$-values, but
from \textbf{direction consistency}: across 34 fold-level
$\Gamma$-versus-nominal comparisons, \textbf{31 better, 3 tied, 0 worse}. The
effect never reverses. Note that the three $z$ values are \emph{not} independent
replications --- same data, nested contracts.

\subsection{The mechanism does not survive scrutiny}
\label{sec:mechanism}

This is the most serious finding, and it argues \emph{against} the tidy story.

\paragraph{$\Gamma$ is slack where the winning layouts sit.}
Fraction of station-folds with $\gbind\le4$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Arm & $z=3$ & $z=4$ & $z=6$ & median $\gbind$ \\
\midrule
$g0$ & 75.0\% & 61.5\% & 46.9\% & 1 / 2 / 8 \\
$g1$ & \textbf{5.2\%} & \textbf{5.2\%} & \textbf{0\%} & 13 / 18 / 49 \\
$g2$ & \textbf{4.2\%} & \textbf{4.2\%} & \textbf{0\%} & 12 / 21 / 49 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The $\Gamma=1$ arm did not land on a layout that $\Gamma=1$ constrains --- it
landed on one protected to $\gbind\approx13$--$49$ at the median station.

\paragraph{$\Gamma\le4$ could not have covered the realised overshoots.}
For $g2$ at $z=3$, residual overshoots require $\gcover\in[5,52]$:
\textbf{0\% coverable at $\Gamma\le4$}.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Both ends fail.} The constraint is slack where the layout sits, and the
failures that remain are ones the budget could never have absorbed. Whatever
produced fewer violated days, \emph{it was not Bertsimas--\c{S}im scenario
coverage.} The defensible statement is that $\Gamma$ acts as a
\textbf{conservatism dial that forces rebalancing} --- displacing the search
away from the concentrated nominal optimum --- not that it insures the
scenarios it nominally insures.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{The binary metric reverses when read continuously}

The headline uses a $0/1$ threshold: did \emph{any} station exceed its band on
day $d$? The \textbf{continuous} version of the identical quantity ---
\texttt{mean\_daily\_ratio}, the mean over days of the worst station's
load/allowance ratio --- tells the opposite story:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
$z=6$, by fold & f0 & f1 & f2 & f3 \\
\midrule
incumbent & 0.783 & 0.817 & 0.811 & 0.774 \\
$g2$      & 0.837 & 0.883 & 0.860 & 0.808 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Every optimised arm runs closer to the band than the incumbent in 3--4
of 4 folds, at every $z$.} Worst-day ratios can also be worse: at $z=3$, $g1$
peaks at \textbf{1.469} versus the incumbent's 1.249 while ``winning'' 3/18 to
6/18.

\textbf{Interpretation.} Optimised layouts \emph{consume the headroom the
contract grants them}, which is what an optimiser is supposed to do. But it
means ``fewer violated days'' and ``more operating margin'' are different
claims, and only the first is true.

\subsection{The seed replication (Gate Z20)}

\paragraph{The objection.} One local-search run per cell. A metaheuristic's
output is stochastic, so a 4-versus-8-day difference could reflect
\emph{where the search stopped} rather than the model. Supporting evidence:
$\beta{=}1.02$ differs from $\Gamma=0$ by 0.215 percentage points of visits at
$z=4$ yet halves violations.

\paragraph{The experiment.} Hexaly exposes a search seed. It was plumbed
through the backend and driver, and $z=4$ was re-run for $\Gamma\in\{0,2\}$ on
all four folds at seeds 11 and 22. With the original grid run this gives
\textbf{three independent samples per cell} --- 8 solves, 2\,h\,50\,m.

\paragraph{Result --- test days violated of 18, pooled over folds:}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Arm & run 1 & run 2 & run 3 & range \\
\midrule
$\Gamma=0$ & 8 & 10 & 7 & \textbf{3} \\
$\Gamma=2$ & 1 & 1  & 2 & \textbf{1} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The distributions are disjoint.} The worst $\Gamma=2$ run still beats
the best $\Gamma=0$ run. Between-arm gap \textbf{7.0 days} against a largest
within-arm range of \textbf{3}. Per (fold, seed) pair, $\Gamma=2$ is better in
\textbf{11 of 12}, tied in the twelfth, and \textbf{never worse}. The $\Gamma$
effect survives the objection.
\end{keybox}

The replication also produced a \emph{measured} noise floor for visits, rather
than one inferred from $\beta$-inversions:
\[
  \text{seed-only visit spread} \;=\; 0.87\%\ (\Gamma{=}0),
  \qquad 0.32\%\ (\Gamma{=}2).
\]

Applied symmetrically:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcl}
\toprule

\midrule
$z=4$, $\Gamma{=}2$ saves vs incumbent & 3.25\% & clears \\

\section{Defensible Claims, Retractions, and Open Problems}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{What can be claimed rigorously}

\paragraph{On the contract redesign --- the strongest result, and a modelling
one rather than a statistical one.}

\begin{keybox}
The revealed-peak ceiling was \emph{provably} unusable: at $q<\max$ the station
ceilings summed to less than the busiest training day, so two of three declared
quantiles described an empty feasible set, and the remaining slack could not
fund $\Gamma\ge1$ on three of four folds. The volume-normalised share band
removes this by construction --- $\sum_s(\mu_s+z\sigma_s)=1+z\sum_s\sigma_s>1$
guarantees aggregate feasibility --- and it is what first made $\Gamma\ge2$
solvable at all. \textbf{This needs no statistics; it is arithmetic,
independently verified.}
\end{keybox}

\paragraph{On the effect of $\Gamma$.}
Layouts solved at $\Gamma\in\{1,2\}$ violate the share band on fewer test days
than the nominal $\Gamma=0$ layout, at every declared $z$, in 31 of 34
fold-level comparisons with zero reversals, and by a margin exceeding the
solver's own run-to-run spread (disjoint distributions over three seeds).
Nearly all the benefit arrives by $\Gamma=1$--$2$; $\Gamma=4$ adds nothing while
costing 3--4 further points of $\PoR$.

\paragraph{On the comparison with the incumbent.}
At $z=4$ and $z=6$, $\Gamma=2$ \textbf{matches} the incumbent's reliability
(1/18 vs 2/18; 1/18 vs 1/18 --- statistical ties, $p\ge0.51$) while using
\textbf{3.3\%--5.8\% fewer station visits}, a margin clearing the measured
0.87\% seed-noise floor.

Note the shape of the claim: \emph{equal reliability at lower cost}, not
superiority on both axes.

\subsection{Remaining limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The mechanism is unexplained.} $\Gamma$ works, but not through
        the channel the theory describes. The honest framing is an indirect
        rebalancing dial.
  \item \textbf{No cost-matched control.} $\beta$ costs 0.4--1.2\%; $\Gamma$
        costs 7--15\%. They are an order of magnitude apart, so ``does $\Gamma$
        beat $\beta$?'' is confounded with ``does spending more help?''
  \item \textbf{Power is capped at $p=0.125$} on weekly units. This cannot be
        fixed by analysis, only by more data.
  \item \textbf{The contract partly encodes the incumbent's policy.} $\mu_s$
        legitimately captures station heterogeneity (a 1{,}700$\times$ speed
        range means equal shares would be \emph{wrong}), but $\sigma_s$ is the
        incumbent's own erraticness --- a policy artefact. At $z=3$,
        \textbf{6 of 24} stations have their entire allowance below an equal
        $1/24$ share, so ``violated days'' measures departure from historical
        practice, not certified physical failure.
  \item \textbf{Generalisation is narrow.} One site, 2{,}000 SKUs, 24 stations,
        11 consecutive weeks, one solver, one incumbent policy, no seasonal
        coverage.
  \item \textbf{No arm is certified within 77\% of optimal.} Every $\PoR$ figure
        is a difference between heuristic incumbents.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future research avenues}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The cost-matched $\beta$ arm} ($\beta\approx1.15$--$1.20$,
        calibrated to cost $\approx$6\%). Settles whether Bertsimas--\c{S}im
        earns its complexity. Cheapest and most decisive experiment available.
  \item \textbf{Extend the horizon.} 51 distinct training days and 4 test weeks
        are the binding constraint on every statistical claim. More weeks buy
        more than any modelling refinement.
  \item \textbf{Chase the mechanism.} If $\Gamma$ works by displacing the search
        rather than by coverage, test that directly: compare against a
        random-restart or diversity-penalised $\Gamma=0$ arm at matched cost. If
        those reproduce the effect, the Bertsimas--\c{S}im interpretation
        collapses and something more interesting is happening.
  \item \textbf{Replace $\sigma_s$ with an engineered capability estimate}, so
        the band measures physical capacity rather than historical behaviour.
  \item \textbf{Seed replication across the whole grid}, not only $z=4$.
\end{enumerate}

% =====================================================================
\section*{Methodological Lessons Worth Carrying Forward}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Methodological Lessons Worth Carrying Forward}
% =====================================================================

\begin{description}[style=unboxed,leftmargin=0pt]

\item[Write the acceptance test before the run, and prove it can fail.]
Gates Z11, Z12, Z15, Z16 and Z17 were authored before any grid data existed.
Z12 in particular caught nothing --- but it was the only reason a layout
breaching its own training contract could not ship silently, which is exactly
what made an earlier exact-solver run meaningless (53--92\% of training days
broken while reported feasible).

\item[A green gate is not evidence until its mutation test is verified.]
Z10 passed for a full cycle while its fixture pointed at the wrong directory.

\item[Commit every phase.]
Twice, tooling written for this study destroyed committed evidence. Both times
it was recoverable \emph{only} because the phase had been committed first.

\item[When a check fails, diagnose before touching it.]
Z9's failure was real, and following it produced a genuine finding --- a
measured suboptimality floor --- rather than a suppressed inconvenience.
Conversely, Z7's \emph{passing} concealed a defect: it measured the wrong day,
and correcting it forecast exactly which grid cells would fail.

\item[Report the counterweight with equal weight.]
The incumbent's continuous-margin advantage, the broken mechanism story, and the
two unsolved arms belong in the abstract, not a footnote. The finding that
survives adversarial review is worth more than the one that merely sounds
better.

\end{description}
