\title{Out-of-Sample Workload Feasibility in CSLAP Layouts:\\
an Empirical Study and a Budgeted-Uncertainty Robust Counterpart}
\author{CSLAP research pipeline}
\date{July 2, 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
We audit the workload-feasibility semantics of the CSLAP evaluation pipeline and re-analyze
every stored layout of the project's instance families (iscf480, iscf10kt, iscfco,
planted-affinity, BERNER). The as-run out-of-sample check compares realized test workload
against a \emph{train-volume-calibrated} ceiling; its bindingness $B$ collapses from
$\approx 1.0$ down to $0.11$--$0.13$ across expanding temporal cuts, so late-cut
``\texttt{wl\_broken}=0'' verdicts are volume slack, not robustness. Under a
volume-normalized ceiling the picture reverses: every temporal fold carries 1--3 of 8
stations above the fair-share ceiling at $k{=}1$ (1--4 over the full closure grid), with
peak load ratios up to 2.52 at $k{=}1$ (2.63 over the full grid; 3.45 in the sparse-train
variants), while the cross-validation folds remain balanced to the exact 10\%-slack edge. A retrospective
Bertsimas--Sim calibration shows the realized overloads sit in the budget-uncertainty sweet
spot: median $\Gamma^{\mathrm{cover}}$ of 8/60 and 19/541 assigned products, deviations
head-concentrated (median Gini 0.63/0.82), and $\Gamma^{\mathrm{bind}}=1$ at most stations.
We derive the exact linear robust counterpart of the workload constraint and implement it
(HiGHS plus a deterministic local search, paired over the temporal cuts). Full-magnitude
protection provably does not fit the 10\%-slack capacity contract (infeasibility
certificates at $\Gamma \ge 4$); ex-ante half-magnitude protection at $\Gamma = 1$ cuts the
out-of-sample peak ratio from 1.77 to 1.59 and the excess workload from 17.4\% to 13.0\%
for a 2.3\% visit price, while uniform ceiling-tightening at the same price buys no peak
reduction at all --- targeted budgeted protection dominates its nominal competitor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and context}
\label{sec:intro}

The Correlated Storage Location Assignment Problem (CSLAP) assigns products
$p \in P$ to picking stations $s \in S$ (binary $x_{ps}$) so as to minimize the total
number of station visits over a set of orders, subject to a slot-capacity constraint
$\sum_p x_{ps} \le \zeta_s$ and a workload constraint
$\sum_p x_{ps} L_p / V_s \le T_s$ per station. The project's preceding research arc took
the covering-enlargement hypothesis H1 --- replace each order by its pattern-closure and
optimize placement on the enlarged instance to buy out-of-sample robustness \emph{for the
visits objective} --- end-to-end through an exact CPLEX re-validation, and falsified it:
the price of robustness is real and the out-of-sample gain is negative, with confidence
intervals excluding zero.\footnote{Internal reports
\texttt{docs/background/7\_scientific\_report\_iscf\_cplex.md} through
\texttt{docs/background/11\_berner\_decomposition.md} in this repository.}

The same arc, however, surfaced one instability in the data that is \emph{not} an artifact
of the covering mechanism: \emph{demand drift}. The drift-characteristics and order-data
diagnostics\footnote{\texttt{docs/background/8\_drift\_characteristics.md} and
\texttt{docs/background/9\_order\_data\_diagnostics.md}.} showed that per-product line counts move
substantially between a training window and a later operating window, while the placement
pipeline calibrates its workload ceiling once, on the training window, and never revisits
it. This study therefore opens a different channel from H1: not the visits objective, but
\textbf{out-of-sample workload feasibility}. The research question is:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Does a CSLAP layout that satisfies the (hard, train-enforced) workload constraint
remain workload-feasible on later demand, and if not, what deviation magnitudes and
protection budgets would a budgeted-uncertainty robust workload constraint have needed?}
\end{quote}

Contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pipeline audit} (\cref{sec:pipeline}): precise semantics of the as-run
feasibility check, and a \emph{bindingness} index $B$ showing the check degenerates into a
volume-calibration test on expanding temporal cuts.
\item \textbf{Volume-normalized reanalysis} (\cref{sec:empirical}): a counterfactual
fair-share ceiling that removes the volume artifact, applied to all stored layouts across
six ISCF-derived families plus the industrial BERNER cell; every recomputed as-run count
reproduces the stored \texttt{wl\_broken} exactly (276 result cells checked across
families), and the train-side constraint held in every one of the $>1{,}000$
station-checks --- so all reported violations are genuinely out-of-sample.
\item \textbf{Retrospective Bertsimas--Sim calibration} (\cref{sec:gamma}): three
deviation-magnitude models (M1 proportional, M2 square-root, M3 train-internal empirical),
fitted coverage constants, and the diagnostics $\Gamma^{\mathrm{cover}}$ /
$\Gamma^{\mathrm{bind}}$ quantifying how large a budget the observed drift asks for and
how small a budget already forces retraining.
\item \textbf{Robust counterpart} (\cref{sec:theory}): the exact linear
Bertsimas--Sim counterpart \cite{bertsimas2004} of the CSLAP workload constraint, in the
row-wise framework of \cite{bental2009robust}, with the Soyster box model
\cite{soyster1973convex} as its $\Gamma \to \zeta_s$ limit.
\item \textbf{Implementation and paired temporal experiment} (\cref{sec:experiment}): the
counterpart implemented on HiGHS with a deterministic local-search incumbent pipeline and
evaluated, paired arm-by-arm, over the five temporal cuts --- yielding infeasibility
certificates for full-magnitude protection under the 10\%-slack contract, a
price--severity frontier for partial insurance, and the head-to-head result that
budgeted protection dominates equal-price uniform ceiling-tightening on out-of-sample
peak severity.
\end{itemize}

\section{The current evaluation pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\paragraph{Placement (train side).}
Layouts are produced by the Hexaly placement model, which enforces the workload
constraint \emph{hard} on training data:\footnote{\texttt{milp\_synthetic.py}, lines
70--77; the line counts are passed by the harness at lines 427 and 441 of
\texttt{run\_robustness\_iscf.py}.} for every station $s$,
$\sum_{p} (L_p / V_s)\, x_{ps} \le T_s$, where $L_p$ is the product's training-window line
count (\texttt{REAL\_LINES}, distinct orders containing $p$). Our recomputation confirms the
constraint held on the training data for every stored layout of every family
(\texttt{viol\_train}=0 in all 800 iscf10kt station-rows, all 160 gamma-family checks, and
all 1{,}088 secondary station-rows).

\paragraph{Ceiling calibration.}
The time capacity is set once per fold from training volume by the 10\%-slack rule
(\texttt{run\_robustness\_iscf.py}, line 211; same formula in
\texttt{iscf\_adapter.py}, line 194):
\begin{equation}
T_s = \Bigl\lceil\, \sigma \cdot \frac{\sum_{p} L^{\mathrm{tr}}_p}{V_s\,|S|} \,\Bigr\rceil,
\qquad \sigma = 1.10,
\label{eq:slackrule}
\end{equation}
with $V_s$ the station speed ($V_s = 1$, uniform, on all ISCF families;
$\sigma = 1.20$ and heterogeneous per-station ceilings on BERNER).

\paragraph{Evaluation (test side).}
The test instance re-uses the \emph{train} stations file
verbatim.\footnote{\texttt{run\_robustness\_iscf.py}, lines 229--233.} The evaluator
(\texttt{evaluate\_layout\_robust.py}, lines 129--142, driven at
\texttt{run\_robustness\_iscf.py}, lines 490--504) computes the test demand
$\ell^{\mathrm{te}}_p$ of each product as its raw test line count, i.e.\
\texttt{groupby('PRODUCT').size()} over the test orders; the realized
workload $W^{\mathrm{te}}_s = \sum_{p \in \Phi_s} \ell^{\mathrm{te}}_p / V_s$ over the
assigned set $\Phi_s$ of the stored layout (a flat JSON dict
\{\texttt{PROD\_<id>}: \texttt{GARE<n>}\}); and the count
$\texttt{wl\_broken} = |\{s : W^{\mathrm{te}}_s > T_s\}|$ (strict inequality) and
\texttt{cap\_broken} analogously against the slot count. Temporal cuts are 5 expanding-train
splits at train fractions $0.5, 0.6, \dots, 0.9$ by \texttt{ORDER\_ID} rank
(train-early/test-late); the cuts share train mass and are a dispersion proxy, not
independent replicates.

\paragraph{The volume artifact.}
The as-run check thus compares \emph{test-window} workload against a
\emph{train-volume-calibrated} ceiling. Define the \textbf{bindingness} of the check as
\begin{equation}
B \;=\; \frac{\sum_p \ell^{\mathrm{te}}_p}{\sum_{s} V_s\, T_s},
\label{eq:bindingness}
\end{equation}
the ratio of total test volume to total ceiling capacity. With uniform speeds and a flat
$T_s$, a layout balanced exactly to the slack edge (peak load $= \sigma \times$
fair share) crosses $T_s$ if and only if $B > 1/\sigma \approx 0.909$; below that threshold
the check cannot fire on any near-balanced layout, regardless of how unbalanced the demand
mix has become. On the expanding temporal design the test window shrinks as the cut moves
late, so $B$ decays mechanically (from $1.005$ to $0.125$ on iscf480) and the as-run
verdict degenerates into a measurement of window length. Conversely, when the test window
carries \emph{more} volume than the train window (sparse-train and affinity families,
$B > 1$), even a perfectly balanced layout must violate, and the as-run count saturates at
8/8 for reasons that have nothing to do with layout quality.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Let $L^{\mathrm{tr}} = \sum_p L^{\mathrm{tr}}_p$ and
$L^{\mathrm{te}} = \sum_p \ell^{\mathrm{te}}_p$ be total train and test lines, and
$\lambda = L^{\mathrm{tr}} / L^{\mathrm{te}}$ the volume ratio. Consistent with the
notation of \cref{sec:theory}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{As-run check}: $W^{\mathrm{te}}_s > T_s$; count
$\texttt{viol\_asrun} = \texttt{wl\_broken}$.
\item \textbf{Normalized check}: the volume-normalized load is
$W^{\mathrm n}_s = \lambda\, W^{\mathrm{te}}_s$
(equivalently, the raw load is compared against the counterfactual test-volume ceiling
$T^{\mathrm n}_s = \lceil \sigma L^{\mathrm{te}} / (V_s |S|) \rceil$; the two violation
sets coincide on every audited fold). We report
$\texttt{viol\_norm} = |\{s : W^{\mathrm n}_s > T_s\}|$ and the peak ratio
$\mathrm{ratio}^{\mathrm n} = \max_s W^{\mathrm{te}}_s / T^{\mathrm n}_s$.
\item \textbf{Imbalance}: $\max_s W^{\mathrm{te}}_s \big/ \bigl(L^{\mathrm{te}} / (V_s |S|)\bigr)$,
the peak-to-fair-share ratio; values $\le \sigma = 1.10$ are within the slack rule.
\item \textbf{Bindingness} $B$ as in \cref{eq:bindingness}; volume-binding threshold
$1/\sigma \approx 0.909$.
\item \textbf{Excess fraction} $X = \sum_s (W^{\mathrm n}_s - T_s)_+ \big/ \sum_s W^{\mathrm n}_s$,
the share of (normalized) test workload sitting above the ceiling.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Sanity.}
The recomputation reproduces the stored \texttt{wl\_broken} in \emph{all} cells: 20
(split, fold, $k$) cells on iscf480, 100 (variant, fold, $k$) rows on iscf10kt, 136
secondary cells (20 iscfco base + 36 affinity + 80 iscfco demand-shift), and the 20 cells
of the $\Gamma$-calibration re-run; recomputed peak workloads match the stored
\texttt{max\_workload} to $<10^{-6}$ (e.g., iscf480 temporal fold 0, $k=1$:
$86{,}019$ vs $T_s = 45{,}365$). \texttt{cap\_broken}=0 everywhere.

\textbf{The CV anchor is a volume artifact.} \Cref{tab:cv480} shows that the headline
``\texttt{wl\_broken}=0 on all CV folds'' says nothing about balance: with
$B \approx 0.227$--$0.228$ against a threshold of $0.909$ (\cref{fig:bindingness}), the
train-calibrated ceiling is
roughly $4\times$ too loose for the CV test volume. The normalized check does surface
violations in 4 of 5 folds at $k{=}1$ --- because Hexaly balances the training workload to
exactly the 10\%-slack edge (imbalance 1.09--1.12 across all (fold, $k$)), any drift tips
the peak station over --- but they are \emph{marginal}: peak ratio at most 1.016, excess
fraction at most $0.40\%$. The honest CV statement is ``balanced to the slack edge, no
material overload.''

\textbf{Temporal drift is severe.} Under the temporal design (\cref{tab:temporal480}),
every cut has 2--3 of 8 stations above the normalized ceiling at $k{=}1$ (2--4 over both
$k$), with peak load
ratios 1.71--2.52 and $X = 11.0$--$30.7\%$ of total test workload above the ceiling;
imbalance reaches 2.77 (cut r0f3, $k{=}1$). Meanwhile the as-run count decays
$4, 2, 1, 0, 0$ in lockstep with $B$ ($1.005 \to 0.125$; \cref{fig:violations}): the late
cuts look ``clean''
as-run exactly where the true overloads are worst. Only cut r0f0 is volume-binding
($B = 1.005$), and there the as-run and normalized checks agree (4 vs 3 stations). The
identity of the peak station changes across cuts (GARE10, GARE4, GARE11, GARE11, GARE6 at
$k{=}1$; \cref{fig:heatmap}; top-3 detail in
\texttt{results/iscf480/top3\_overloaded\_temporal.csv}); every
station holds exactly 60 products in every layout (slot capacity fully binding), so the
overloads are pure demand-mix drift, not slot-count effects. The $k{=}2$ closure layouts
are consistently \emph{less extreme} at the peak (mean peak ratio 1.86 vs 2.15; lower in
all 5 cuts) but remain far infeasible, and do not reduce the violation \emph{count}
(cuts r0f1, r0f3).

\subsection{Industrial cell: BERNER audit}
\label{sec:berner}

BERNER is the one heterogeneous family: 24 stations with per-station
$T_s \in \{1, \dots, 158\}$ (calibrated at 120\% of the pinned-assignment train workload,
verified exactly for all 24 stations), speeds 332.6--11{,}885.7, and a single temporal cut
(train quantile 0.7692, 10 of 13 weeks; 778{,}788 raw train lines, 220{,}893 raw test
lines). No layouts were persisted, so realized per-station test loads cannot be
recomputed; the audit instead bounds the check's informativeness four ways, all agreeing:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Aggregate bindingness}:
$B_{\mathrm{agg}}^{\mathrm{te}} = 220{,}893 / 898{,}472 = 0.246$, far below the
$1/1.20 = 0.833$ threshold (the 3-week test window carries 30.1\% of the distinct-line
\texttt{REAL\_LINES} train volume of $734{,}229$ --- the basis that calibrates $T_s$ ---
and 28.4\% of the 778{,}788 raw train lines). The stored test-demand
\texttt{wl\_broken}=0 for the deployable arms A/B (all 16
decompose rows, every $k$) is therefore \emph{volume-trivial}.
\item \textbf{Assignment bounds}: under the pinned identity assignment the worst station
reaches only ratio 0.286 on test demand (train-frequency-proportional split: 0.251) --- no
train-like layout could plausibly have fired the as-run test check.
\item \textbf{Train side, the contract is binding}:
$B_{\mathrm{agg}}^{\mathrm{tr}} = 734{,}229 / 898{,}472 = 0.817 \approx 1/1.20$ (the
train side of the aggregate bindingness uses the pinned-assignment
\texttt{REAL\_LINES} workload, the quantity $T_s$ was calibrated on; the 778{,}788 raw
train lines would give 0.867), pinned train ratios reach 0.8333
at every large station, and the hard-constrained clean-comparison arms sit at
\texttt{real\_wl\_max\_ratio} 0.998--1.000 --- placement is \emph{at} the ceiling.
\item \textbf{Shape-bindingness}: a fluid equal-workload split of test demand
($W^\ast = 5.674$ per station) would overload station 12 ($T_s = 1$) by $5.67\times$ and
station 24 ($T_s = 2$) by $2.84\times$: equal balancing is incompatible with the
heterogeneous per-station contract, so the constraint binds in shape as well as volume.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion and recommendations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{When is the robust workload constraint worth it?}
The evidence separates three regimes.
(i) \emph{Stationary demand, matched volume} (iscf480 CV): the nominal constraint is
adequate --- normalized violations exist only because placement sits at the exact slack
edge, and they are immaterial (peak ratio $\le 1.016$, $X \le 0.40\%$). Robustness would
buy little here.
(ii) \emph{Temporal drift} (all temporal families, and the planted demand shift): the
nominal constraint fails materially and systematically --- 1--4 of 8 stations, peak ratios
up to 2.52 on iscf480 and 2.63 on the full-catalogue $k$ grid (3.45 in the sparse-train
variants), 7--37\% of workload above the fair-share ceiling --- and the failure is invisible
to the as-run check whenever $B < 1/\sigma$. This is the regime the counterpart of
\cref{sec:theory} targets, and the calibration says protection is cheap in budget terms:
the realized overloads are covered at median $\Gamma^{\mathrm{cover}}$ of 8/60 and 19/541
products under the best-fitting magnitude model --- strikingly close to the
$\Gamma \sim \sqrt{n}$ rule of thumb of \cref{rem:bs-prob}
($\sqrt{60} \approx 7.7$, $\sqrt{541} \approx 23.3$) --- and deviations are
head-concentrated (Gini 0.63/0.82), the Bertsimas--Sim sweet spot.
(iii) \emph{Volume-artifact regimes} ($B \gg 1$ or $B \ll 1/\sigma$): fix the protocol
first; no constraint reformulation can rescue a check that compares incommensurate volumes.

\paragraph{What $\Gamma$ and what magnitudes the evidence suggests.}
A per-station budget of order $\sqrt{\zeta_s}$ (reported as the fraction
$\Gamma_s / \zeta_s \approx 13\%$ and $4\%$ on the two families) would have covered the
median realized overload. Because $\Gamma^{\mathrm{bind}} = 1$ at 58--68\% of stations,
even $\Gamma = 1$ already reshapes the training solution --- the practical risk is
over-conservatism, not vacuity, which argues for starting at small budgets and the
fractional-budget refinement rather than box protection. The harder open problem is the
\emph{magnitude} side: the required $c_1(0.9) \approx 1.2$--$1.6$ (growing with horizon)
is far beyond what train-internal variability (M3) predicts on iscf480, so an ex-ante
deviation model needs longer demand history or an explicit drift model, not just
within-window dispersion; on the full-catalogue instance M3 is a usable starting point
(38--80\% coverage).

\paragraph{What the implementation shows.}
The computational study of \cref{sec:experiment} turns the counterpart from a proposal
into three practical statements. (a) \emph{Partial ex-ante insurance is cheap and
effective on severity}: $\Gamma = 1$ at half the M3 magnitude cuts the out-of-sample peak
by ten percent of its value and the excess workload by a quarter, for a
$2.3\%$ visit price, sign-consistently across all five cuts. (b) \emph{Uniform
tightening is not a substitute}: at the same visit price it leaves the peak where it was,
because it rebalances nominal loads without separating the large deviators that create
the peak --- the budget constraint does that separation by construction. (c)
\emph{Full-magnitude protection is a capacity decision, not a modeling one}: the top
M3 deviations alone exceed the whole cell's slack headroom ($48.6$k vs.\ $33.0$k lines
on the first cut), HiGHS certifies infeasibility from $\Gamma = 4$ up, and the one
constructible step beyond the headroom ($\Gamma = 2$, $\alpha = 0.5$, one cut) costs
$19\%$ of train visits. Insuring the drift this data actually exhibits requires growing
the slack $\sigma$ --- a capacity investment that \cref{sec:exp-impl} prices.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
(i) The five temporal cuts share train mass (expanding windows): their spread is a
dispersion proxy, not a confidence interval, and per-fold results are correlated.
(ii) The volume-normalized ceiling is a \emph{counterfactual protocol choice}
($T^{\mathrm n}$ re-solves nothing; it asks how the trained layout would fare if the
ceiling had been calibrated on the test volume). Other normalizations are defensible; the
$B$ diagnosis of the as-run check does not depend on this choice.
(iii) Layouts are Hexaly (metaheuristic) placements: balancing exactly to the slack edge
is a solver behavior, and better or different placements could shift the borderline CV
findings (though hardly the 1.7--2.5$\times$ temporal overloads).
(iv) Instance breadth: all synthetic families derive from one warehouse's catalogue, and
the industrial evidence is a single BERNER cell with a single temporal cut and no persisted
layouts (its bindingness verdict rests on assignment bounds).
(v) The $\Gamma$ calibration is retrospective by design; only M3 is available ex ante, and
it underestimates drift on iscf480 by an order of magnitude.
(vi) The experiment's incumbents are metaheuristic, with a $\approx 30\%$ HiGHS dual-bound
gap on the truncated support objective; paired within-cut deltas are reliable, absolute
PoR magnitudes less so.
(vii) The partial-insurance grid is coarse ($\alpha \in \{0.5, 0.25\}$), and the
infeasible robust arms at $\alpha < 1$ are undetermined-within-budget rather than proven
infeasible; only the full-magnitude certificates at $\Gamma \in \{4, 8, 16\}$ are proofs.
